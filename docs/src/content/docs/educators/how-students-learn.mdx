QuestLab is built on one idea about learning: **students build their own understanding**. They do it by acting on the world, noticing what happens, and making sense of it. Being told is not enough. This idea is called **constructivism**, and it comes from the psychologist **Jean Piaget**. His student and colleague **Seymour Papert** built on it with **constructionism**: people build ideas best when they are building something real that they can share.

Everything else in QuestLab grows from these two ideas. The worlds to walk around in, the experiments, the questions that come before the explanation, and student building are all there for the same reason. The [game design rules](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/) apply them to missions, and the AI generator designs every mission under them.

<Aside type="note" title="In short">
- **Piaget (constructivism):** students build ideas by doing, and they change their minds when what they see doesn't fit what they expected. What they can reason about grows with age.
- **Papert (constructionism):** students learn most when they make something of their own that others can see, use or play.
- **In QuestLab:** do first, name it after. Let a surprise do the teaching. Fit the reasoning to the grade. End with something students made.
</Aside>

## Piaget: students build their own ideas

Piaget spent his career watching how children think. He found that children are not empty containers waiting to be filled. They are always building a working picture of how the world works, and they test it against what happens.

### New things fit, or the picture changes

When something new fits a student's picture, they take it in easily. Piaget called this *assimilation*. When it doesn't fit, they feel puzzled and off balance, and they have to change the picture itself. He called this *accommodation*. That uncomfortable moment, which he called *disequilibrium*, is where real learning happens.

So a lesson that only confirms what students already think teaches little. A lesson that lets them predict, look, and find out *they were wrong* teaches a lot, as long as it is safe to be wrong and someone helps them build the better idea.

**In QuestLab:**
- A **prediction question** comes before the experiment, so students commit to what they think. The report keeps their first answer.
- The world **behaves the way the real thing does**. When the heavy ball and the light ball land together, the surprise does the work, not a paragraph of text.
- A character then helps students **work out why**, by asking rather than lecturing.
- Wrong answers say why and loop back. Being wrong is cheap, so students are willing to risk a guess.

### Doing comes before naming

For Piaget, knowledge starts in action: handling things, sorting them, changing them and watching what happens. The word for an idea makes sense only once there is an experience for it to stick to.

**In QuestLab:** students mix the solutions, then learn the word *acid*. They watch the pollen travel, then hear *pollination*. A glossary word or an infobox explains something students have already met. It never comes before it.

### Thinking grows in stages

Piaget described stages in how children reason. The ages are only a rough guide, and children vary. Later research found that many children can do more than Piaget expected, sooner, when a task is familiar to them. Still, the stages are a good way to judge what kind of reasoning a mission can ask for.

Grades | Piaget's stage (roughly) | What fits in a mission
------ | ------------------------ | ----------------------
Pre-K to 1 | Preoperational, moving to concrete (about 4 to 7) | Things students can see and touch. One feature at a time (young children often think the taller glass "has more"). Show changes happening rather than asking students to imagine them. Sorting and matching by one feature. Cause and effect they can watch. No hidden variables and no symbols standing for amounts.
2 to 5 | Concrete operational (about 7 to 11) | Logical reasoning about real things. Sorting by two features, putting things in order, measuring and comparing. Seeing that a change can be undone. A fair test that changes one thing. Every abstract idea tied to something in the world that students can act on.
6 to 8 | Formal thinking emerging (about 11 to 14) | Hypotheses and what-ifs, controlling variables, proportions. But many students still need something concrete first: start each new idea with something to handle or watch, then ask them to predict, generalize and explain why.
9 to 12 | Formal operational (about 14 and up) | Abstract reasoning: hypotheses, several variables, systematic tests, arguing from evidence about things they cannot see. Still, introduce each new idea with a concrete case before the general rule.

For a class that spans stages, design for the youngest and let a **Bonus** objective stretch the oldest.

### Classmates help, too

Piaget saw that children often change their minds more readily when a classmate disagrees with them than when an adult corrects them. Between equals, they have to argue it out. That is one reason QuestLab puts students in teams in one shared world, and asks the group to predict before it tests.

## Papert: learning by making

Seymour Papert worked with Piaget in Geneva, then went to MIT. He agreed that students build their own understanding, and he added that they do it best **while building something outside their heads**: a program, a model, a machine, a story, something other people can look at, use and talk about. He called this *constructionism*.

Papert helped create the Logo programming language, so children could teach a "turtle" to draw. He found that while they made the turtle do what they wanted, they were learning geometry. A few of his ideas matter most for QuestLab:

- **Making something real gives the learning a purpose.** Students care about a fox's habitat when they are building one for it.
- **Things to think with.** A model you made is something you can reason with: change it, test it, explain it to someone else.
- **Mistakes are bugs to fix, not failures.** When a build doesn't work, the student looks for why and tries again. That debugging *is* the learning.
- **Hard fun.** Students work hardest at something that is difficult *and* theirs.
- **Low floor, high ceiling.** Anyone can start, and there is no limit to how far a student can take it.

**In QuestLab:**
- [Student building](/docs/editor/student-building/) lets students make things that stay in a scene: a habitat, labels on a diagram, a poster from another app, clues hidden for classmates. You approve what they build before others see it.
- A good build prompt asks students to **use the idea**, not to decorate: *"Build a habitat that keeps a desert fox cool, and label what each part does."*
- The best time for a build is **near the end**, once students have met the idea and can show what they understand.
- Still to come: students remixing whole scenarios, and making their own.

## What it means for you

<Host who="val" side="right">
You are not the source of every answer. You are the one who asks the question that gets a stuck team moving again.
</Host>

In a constructivist classroom, the teacher is the guide, not the source of every answer. QuestLab keeps you at the centre of that role:

- **Before:** set up the question the class is chasing. Don't give away the answer.
- **During:** when a team is stuck, ask what they predicted and what they saw. Don't tell them. See [You are in the room](/docs/educators/facilitation/).
- **After:** debrief from their **first answers** and their builds. The report shows where the class's ideas were, so you can build on them the next day. See [Maximizing learning](/docs/educators/maximize-learning/).

## Where this comes from

Jean Piaget (1896–1980) studied how children's thinking develops (*The Origins of Intelligence in Children*, *The Child's Conception of the World*). Seymour Papert (1928–2016) worked with Piaget, then founded the Epistemology and Learning Group at the MIT Media Lab. He described constructionism in *Mindstorms: Children, Computers, and Powerful Ideas* (1980). Later researchers have refined Piaget's stages, and Lev Vygotsky added a stronger place for language and for help from others. Treat the stages as a guide to what usually fits, not as a limit on what a child can do.
