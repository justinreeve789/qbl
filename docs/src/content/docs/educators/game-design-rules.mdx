[Designing missions that teach](/docs/educators/design-for-learning/) starts from the debrief and works backwards: objectives, questions and wrong answers. This page adds the *game* side: how a mission starts, what students do in it, how it helps them when they are stuck, and how teams work together. The rules draw on research into educational games and simulations (see [where they come from](#where-these-rules-come-from)).

All of them serve one mindset. QuestLab is **constructivist**: students build their own understanding by acting on a world and making sense of what happens (Jean Piaget), and they learn most when they make something of their own that others can see (Seymour Papert's constructionism). [How students learn](/docs/educators/how-students-learn/) explains both. When two rules seem to pull apart, choose the version that lets students find out for themselves.

The 27 rules are grouped by theme, and you do not need to follow every one in every mission. Use the [checklist](#a-pre-publish-checklist) at the end as a last pass.

## The first five minutes

### 1. Let students act within two minutes

Long backstories, cutscenes and reading before any action make students lose interest. Keep the opening short, and put something to do on screen as soon as students arrive. Tell the story through what they find, not through what they sit through.

<Aside type="tip" title="Check it">
Time a first-time player. If nothing is interactive after two minutes, cut.
</Aside>

### 2. Make the first scene a safe on-ramp

Use the first scene to teach moving, talking, the bag and the objectives checklist. Do not gate anything and do not penalize mistakes. Introduce one new thing at a time. Students who have never played this kind of game should be able to finish scene 1 without help.

### 3. Start simple, then add layers

Begin with a simplified version of the idea: one variable on the [bench](/docs/editor/bench/), one clear question. Add complexity scene by scene, and use objectives or abilities to open later scenes. Each scene should need only skills taught before it.

### 4. Make it hard, but learnable

Students enjoy a challenge they can overcome, and they do not enjoy a dumbed-down one. Raise the difficulty gradually, and add at least one **Bonus** objective that is harder than the rest for students who want more.

## The learning is the play

<Host who="val" side="right">
A world to walk around in teaches nothing by itself. Give every place something to do.
</Host>

### 5. Give every scene a verb tied to a learning goal

For each scene, say what the student *does* there and which goal it serves ("the lab: test the samples to find the source"). If you cannot, cut the scene or merge it into another. Each learning goal needs at least one action that produces evidence: a checked answer box, a bench goal, a variable the student sets. The report shows how many students have evidence for each goal ([Reading the reports](/docs/editor/reports/)).

### 6. Build the mission from the content, not from points

Stars, XP and badges alone do not change how students learn. Make the challenge, the characters and the story out of the topic itself. Give rewards for objectives and mastery, and never for clicking around or visiting a place. Award each reward once, on an event rather than on a choice (see [stars, XP and the marketplace](/docs/developers/actions/award/)).

<Aside type="tip" title="Check it">
Imagine the mission with no stars or badges. Is it still worth playing?
</Aside>

### 7. Put the information in the world

Let characters, objects, conversations, glossary words and the way an experiment behaves carry the key ideas. Use infoboxes as lab books and references, not as the whole lesson, and do not put a wall of reading in front of an action.

### 8. Make sure the rules are right

A mission teaches through how its system behaves, not only through its text. If a bench or formula is wrong, students learn the wrong thing, whatever the box says. Check [bench](/docs/editor/bench/) kits, formulas and physics against real values. Where you simplify on purpose, say so in the debrief. Also make sure the rewards do not work against the lesson, for example by rewarding speed in a lesson about careful measurement.

<Aside type="tip" title="Check it">
Ask someone who knows the subject to play and try to break the model.
</Aside>

### 9. Give students a role

Name a role: field biologist, ship engineer, historian. Make the tasks use that person's tools, questions and standards of evidence, and let your characters talk to the student as colleagues would. Students learn more from working the way a practitioner works than from being handed facts. Write objectives as what that person would do ("Report the source of the contamination").

### 10. Give them a place to experiment

Include at least one scene where students can change something, watch what happens and try again: a bench, or a box of [hands-on instruments](/docs/editor/infoboxes/#hands-on-instruments) with sliders and switches. Trying again should cost nothing.

## Help and failure

[Designing missions that teach](/docs/educators/design-for-learning/#write-wrong-answers-that-teach) covers questions and wrong answers in depth. Three rules from the game side:

### 11. Give feedback right away, and say why

Slow or vague feedback loses students. When an answer is wrong or a goal is missed, say at once what went wrong and give a nudge toward what to try next. Never leave it at "Wrong." On a [hands-on instrument](/docs/editor/infoboxes/#hands-on-instruments), use its *misses* messages to name the specific reason.

### 12. Make failure cheap and a little fun

Avoid dead ends and game-over moments. A wrong answer or a failed test should loop back to practice, with a light tone and no cost for trying again. Players stay hopeful when failing is safe.

### 13. Layer the help, and let characters give it

Go from a nudge, to a pointer, to a worked example. A coach character is a natural way to do it: a [conversation](/docs/editor/conversations/) can give a small hint the first time and a clearer one when the student comes back (use *Said only when*). Never penalize a student for asking.

## Choice and purpose

### 14. Let students choose their challenge

Offer a choice of order or path where you can, and keep **Bonus** content truly optional. People work hardest on challenges they chose.

### 15. Give the group a goal and a payoff

Use a **Shared** variable (one value for everyone playing together) that each student's work adds to, and show the progress. Include at least one payoff moment: a reveal, a discovery, a badge. A shared goal makes each student's effort matter to the group.

## Working together

### 16. Make teammates need each other

Teams play one world together, so use that. Split information, tools or measurements across players, and use Shared variables so one student's finding unlocks another's task. Do not build a mission one confident student can solve alone.

### 17. Support figuring things out together

Give teams something shared to build on: a graph, a journal, a board in the room. Add prompts such as "What do you predict?" and let evidence settle disagreements. See [Maximizing learning](/docs/educators/maximize-learning/#5-put-students-in-teams-on-purpose).

### 18. Leave room for play

Students enjoy exploring without being scored. Do not attach points to every move, and do not lock the fun behind quizzes. If everything counts, nothing feels like play.

## Debrief and testing

### 19. End with an after-action review

What happened, why, and where does it show up in real life? Close the mission with a reflection moment, and write discussion prompts in the assignment note. [Maximizing learning](/docs/educators/maximize-learning/) shows how to debrief from what students did.

### 20. Expect students to play their own way

Students bring their own goals and their own experience with games. Watch real students play, and use the report to see where they stall, wander off or leave. Teach the controls in scene 1, because experience with games varies.

### 21. Measure more than accuracy

Right answers alone do not show that students learned. Look at what they did (do they use the idea again later?), whether they understand it beyond recognizing it, whether they stayed engaged, and whether the mission was easy to reach and well chunked. In the report, read first answers, number of tries, time, and the scene where students stop. Add at least one question that asks students to explain *why*, as evidence of thinking and not just answers; the game uses explanation choices rather than typed answers. See [Testing](/docs/editor/settings-and-publishing/#testing).

## Fairness and values

### 22. Check the values in your design

Every design choice says something. Ask who your characters are, who leads in team tasks, whose knowledge counts, which avatars and voices appear, and whether any stereotype has slipped in. Use a mix of avatars, and keep reading levels fitted to your students.

### 23. Playtest early and often, with varied players

Watching real play turns accidents into choices. Ask a mix of students to play early drafts, not only the finished mission, and ask them what they notice. The teacher reset and the staff *Start again* let you repeat runs easily.

### 24. Let students make

Students learn most when they make something real that others can see, use or play. Papert called this constructionism. Helping others and changing a game is part of deep engagement, too. In scenes that allow it, students can add pieces, words and their own pictures, and you approve what they build before others see it. See [Student building](/docs/editor/student-building/).

Give the build a task that **uses the idea**, not one that only decorates: *"Build a habitat that keeps a desert fox cool, and label what each part does."* Put it near the end, once students have met the idea. When a build doesn't work, treat it as a bug to fix, not a failure. Remixing whole scenarios is still to come.

## How students build ideas

These three rules come from Piaget's constructivism. See [How students learn](/docs/educators/how-students-learn/) for the theory behind them.

### 25. Explore before you explain

Students meet each idea by doing something with it first: handling, trying, sorting or watching it happen. Only then does a character, an observation or a glossary word give it a name. A word makes sense once there is an experience for it to stick to, so never define a word before students have met the thing it names.

### 26. Let a surprise change their minds

Students change their ideas when what they see doesn't fit what they expected. For each common misconception, set up a moment where the wrong idea predicts the wrong result:

<Steps>
1. Ask for a **prediction** first. Teammates may disagree, which is good.
2. Let students **see or measure** something that doesn't fit the wrong idea.
3. Have a character help them **build a better explanation**, by asking, not telling.
</Steps>

Feedback on an answer that comes from the misconception should point at the evidence they just saw.

<Aside type="tip" title="Check it">
For each misconception you listed, find the moment in the mission where students see it fail.
</Aside>

### 27. Ask for the reasoning the grade can do

What students can reason about grows with age. Younger students reason from what they can see and touch, one feature at a time. In grades 2 to 5 they reason logically about real things they can handle and measure. From about grade 6, they start to handle hypotheses, variables and what-ifs, but many still need something concrete first. Build the puzzles, experiments and questions for your students' stage, and let a **Bonus** objective reach beyond it. The table in [How students learn](/docs/educators/how-students-learn/#thinking-grows-in-stages) gives each stage and what fits.

## A pre-publish checklist

Run this list before you launch. Add it to the one in <em>Designing missions that teach</em>.

- [ ] The first interactive thing appears within two minutes.
- [ ] Scene 1 is a safe on-ramp.
- [ ] Every scene has a verb tied to a learning goal.
- [ ] Students do something with each idea before it is named.
- [ ] Each misconception meets a surprise that students see for themselves.
- [ ] Puzzles and questions fit the reasoning my students' grade can do.
- [ ] Rewards come from mastery, not from clicking.
- [ ] Key facts live in the world, not in walls of text.
- [ ] Someone who knows the subject has checked the experiments and formulas.
- [ ] Failing is cheap, and a wrong answer says why and loops back.
- [ ] Help comes in layers, from a character.
- [ ] At least one **Bonus** objective is optional and harder.
- [ ] Team tasks split information or tools.
- [ ] There is a shared goal and a payoff moment.
- [ ] A debrief moment ends the mission, and at least one question asks students to explain why.
- [ ] I have checked the values in my design.
- [ ] Where it fits, students make something that uses the idea.
- [ ] Real, varied students have played it, early and more than once.

## In the AI generator

The [AI mission generator](/docs/editor/plans-and-generator/#the-ai-mission-generator) follows these rules for you. **Every scenario it makes is designed under them**, missions and stories alike, whichever generation method you use. It applies each rule **where it reasonably fits**: not every rule suits every scenario (a mission about a poem has no instrument to tune, so its place to try things might be a sorting or a what-if conversation instead), and the generator uses its judgement rather than forcing a rule in where it would make the mission worse. A rule that does fit is not skipped just because it is extra work.

- **Its designer** gets the rules with its instructions. A story students watch gets the ones that fit a story: a fast start, simple first then layers, the lesson in the world, accuracy, characters who work like practitioners and make mistakes they recover from, a shared goal and a payoff, a varied cast and a closing discussion that asks why.
- **Before anything is drawn**, it checks the rules a program can count, where they apply to every mission: something to click in the first scene from the start, an opening that is not too long, at least one optional bonus step, a debrief question, no stars for just talking to someone, and a prediction question before any experiment. A mission that breaks one goes back to be fixed.
- **Its reviewer** (in the `critic` and `best-of` [generation methods](/docs/developers/generation-methods/)) checks the mission against the whole list and names any rule it breaks that it could reasonably have followed.
- **The constructivist rules.** It is told QuestLab is constructivist before any rule. The brief for each mission says what thinking stage fits the grade (a range is designed for its youngest). Its designer has students do before it names, sets up a surprise for each misconception it is asked to confront, and asks for reasoning that fits the stage.
- **A scene to build in.** Where it fits, it gives one scene, usually near the end, **Student building** with a task that uses the idea ("Plan a flower bed the bees will love, and label why each flower is there"), the tools that suit it, and the mission's own items as pieces to place. You still approve what students build. Nothing in the mission waits on a build, so it is never needed to finish. You can change or turn off the build in the scene's settings.
- **Teamwork without roles.** Every player can do anything the others can; if you want crew roles, hand them out in class. To make teammates need each other (rule 16), it spreads clues, things to collect and measurements across scenes, so the group has to pool what it finds, and adds team tasks where they fit.

**A single scene** made on its own (**✨ Generate a scene**, outside the scenario generator) is not held to these rules: it is a place to build in, and the rules are about a scenario as a whole.

Checking the science with someone who knows the subject, checking the values and playtesting with real students are still yours: a generated mission is a draft for you to look over.

### Where the rules pull two ways

Some rules pull against each other. Under **Design approach (optional)** on the Generate page you can choose which way a mission leans (`--opening`, `--focus` and `--freedom` on the command line). Each starts at **Choose for me**: while it matches the mission to standards, the generator picks what suits your description, grade and subject. It picks the middle choice unless your description points another way (a story to set the scene, a lab practical for older students). A setting you choose always wins. A generated mission's card shows how it leans, marking what was chosen for you.

Tension | Setting | Choices
------- | ------- | -------
**A fast start, or the story?** Long openings lose players, but a good story pulls them in. | **How it starts** | **Straight into the action** (at most two opening lines). **Action first, story through discoveries** (at most four). **Story first** (still playing within two minutes). Missions only.
**Rigor, or engagement?** A fast hook keeps students; accurate systems and real practice teach them. | **Rigor or play** | **Lighter and playful** (still accurate). **Quick hook, rigorous middle.** **Rigorous, like a practitioner**, from the start. Missions and stories.
**Teacher control, or student choice?** Students steer play their own way whatever we plan. | **Student freedom** | **Guided, one path**, each step opening the next. **Some choice of order** inside a clear goal. **Open to explore**: most of it in any order, more to discover. Missions only.

## Where these rules come from

The mindset behind them comes from Jean Piaget (constructivism: how children build their understanding, and how their reasoning develops) and Seymour Papert (constructionism: learning by making). See [How students learn](/docs/educators/how-students-learn/).

The rules draw on research and writing about educational games and simulations by Clark Aldrich (simulations and virtual worlds), Jane McGonigal (what makes games motivating), James Paul Gee (learning principles in good games), Karl Kapp (building the content itself into the game), Kurt Squire (games as designed experiences in classrooms), Constance Steinkuehler (learning in online game communities), David Williamson Shaffer (learning to think like a professional), Ian Bogost (how a game's rules make an argument) and Mary Flanagan (human values in design).

Much of this research rests on case studies and classroom experience rather than controlled trials, so treat the rules as strong guidelines and test them with your own students.
