```text wrap title="Bridge the Gap at Kessa Gorge: generator prompt"
Make a mission called Bridge the Gap at Kessa Gorge for grades 3 to 5. Teach and assess NGSS 3-5-ETS1-1, 3-5-ETS1-2, 3-5-ETS1-3 and 3-PS2-1 and Common Core 4.OA.A.3. They sort the facts into what the bridge must do and what limits them. At the timber yard a price board drives multistep questions about totals and money left. In the test rig shed an instrument has a switch for a flat deck or a triangle frame and a slider for weight; a meter shows the load until it breaks. Ask for a prediction first. The build yard is a placeholder scene that we will replace by hand with the shared physics bridge builder, so give it only an arrival line and an exit. Invent a twist where the steel is late and the team must redesign with wood and rope. Optional harder objective: the cheapest bridge that holds 100 kg. Build scene in the far village: students place a plaque and label what made their bridge strong. Debrief: why do triangles help?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Gorge Edge.** On-ramp. Look over the drop, inspect the snapped posts, take the measuring rope.
2. **Survey Camp.** Measure the gap, weigh the cart, ask Elder Pema about the money. Sort the facts into the brief.
3. **Timber Yard.** Prices and the maths. The steel is late: the twist lands here.
4. **Test Rig Shed.** Flat against triangle. The triangle frame holds more than twice the load.
5. **Build Yard.** The shared physics scene. Everyone builds on the same bridge. Test with 20 kg, then 100 kg.
6. **Far Village.** Payoff. The cart rolls across. Plaque and debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Criteria or constraint: “must hold 100 kg” is one, “3,000 coins” is the other. A wrong sort gets a reason.
- The first flat deck sags and snaps at the middle joint. Beams turn red before they go, so students see where force gathers.
- A rope placed as a prop goes slack. Ropes only pull, so it must hang from above.
- Fair test: the rig refuses a second change. “You changed the shape and the material. Which one helped?”

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
Hold 100 kg for under 2,000 coins.
</Aside>

## Teacher plan

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Before" icon="comment">
    Sketch a flat plank bridge and a triangle bridge. “Same wood. Which holds more?”
  </Card>
  <Card title="During" icon="laptop">
    Teams of three or four share one bridge. Expect the first three builds to fall; that is on plan. Ask each team what they will change and why before they rebuild.
  </Card>
  <Card title="After" icon="approve-check">
    Teams present cost and load held. Bridge designs are not saved between visits, so have teams screenshot the final build. The report shows the criteria sort and the maths.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Example" icon="star">
    Follow with one real build in craft sticks, using the best design from the game. Students arrive with a plan instead of a guess.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

A craft-stick bridge takes days to glue, gets one destructive test, and fails in a blink with the cause hidden. That is one cycle of the design process, and the standard asks for several. Here a team runs twenty cycles in a lesson, watches beams turn red before they break, and builds on the same structure at the same moment. The budget is enforced by the world, not by the honour system. A lecture or video can explain triangles, but cannot hand a student a failed bridge of their own to fix. The real build still matters and comes after.

</BlueprintWhy>
