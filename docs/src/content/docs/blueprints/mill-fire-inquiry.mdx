```text wrap title="The Mill Fire Inquiry: generator prompt"
Make a mission called The Mill Fire Inquiry for grades 6 to 8. Teach and assess Common Core RH.6-8.1, RH.6-8.6, RH.6-8.8 and RH.6-8.9. Students are junior archivists working with Archivist Roshan at a town museum. A 1954 plaque blames the 1904 Calder Mill fire on the carelessness of the night watchman, Tomasz Wolski. His great-granddaughter asks the museum to look again. Scenes: the museum hall, an archive basement underground, the mill ruins, a newspaper office and the great-granddaughter's porch, where family memory is one more voice to weigh. Seven sources are spread across the scenes so teams split up; each is an item with a short text: the mill owner's letter to his insurer, full of loaded words; the fire brigade log with times and a note that the yard had no water; a newspaper editorial that is all opinion; a mill girl's diary, first-hand but from one room; a payroll ledger showing the watchman on a second sixteen-hour night and the barrels removed on the owner's order; a 1954 town history that copies the owner's letter; and an insurance map showing the fire began far from the watchman's hut. Bag puzzles: a hidden cabinet key, a magnifier for a faded date, two torn letter halves to combine. Questions ask who wrote it and why, what they could not see, and whether a sentence is fact, opinion or reasoned judgment. A timeline exposes a clash: the letter says the watchman slept until ten; the log records the alarm at 9:40. Wrong ideas to surprise: older and official sources are the most reliable; a printed history is independent of the sources it used; if the watchman was not to blame, the owner must have done it. Invent a twist where a lost diary page upsets the team's favourite theory. The best-supported conclusion is modest: cause unknown, the watchman raised the alarm, the missing water made it worse. Build scene: students write the new plaque and add a hotspot citing their best source. Debrief: why did the 1954 book get it wrong?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Museum Hall.** On-ramp. Read the plaque. Meet Mrs. Wolska-Reed, who says only: “That is not the story my family tells.”
2. **Archive Basement.** Lantern light, locked cabinets, the letter in two halves, the ledger.
3. **Newspaper Office.** The editorial and, on a high shelf, the 1954 history.
4. **Mill Ruins.** The insurance map laid over what is left. The watchman's hut is at the wrong end.
5. **The Porch.** Family memory. Students decide what weight it can bear.
6. **The lost page.** The twist. The diarist saw the owner carrying buckets too.
7. **Museum Hall.** Timeline, conclusion, new plaque, debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Loaded words: students pick the five words in the owner's letter that judge instead of describe, then say what he stood to gain.
- Copy or confirmation: the 1954 history and the letter share whole phrases. Two sources that agree may be one source counted twice.
- The clock: the letter's ten o'clock against the log's 9:40 and the diary's half past nine.
- Conclusion: three options, scored by how much evidence each has. Blaming the owner scores lowest of all.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
Find the one fact every source agrees on, and explain why that matters.
</Aside>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

A document packet gives students the sources already chosen, cleaned and stapled in order, with the author and date in a helpful header. Half of a historian's work has been done for them. Here the sources must be found, their makers and motives worked out, and one of them caught copying another. Different teammates hold different documents, so corroboration is a conversation. Few classes can visit an archive, and a lecture can only report someone else's verdict. The mission also lets students be wrong in the tempting direction, by swapping one villain for another, and shows them the evidence does not stretch that far.

</BlueprintWhy>
