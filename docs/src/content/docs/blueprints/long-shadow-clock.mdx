```text wrap title="The Long Shadow Clock: generator prompt"
Make a mission called The Long Shadow Clock for grade 1. Teach and assess NGSS 1-ESS1-1 and 1-ESS1-2 and Common Core 1.MD.B.3, 1.MD.A.1 and 1.MD.C.4. Students are junior sky-watchers helping Clockkeeper Amara fix the village clock tower, which stopped in the night. Setting: a village square with a tall flagpole and a bell. Ringing the bell moves time on. Build the square as separate scenes for morning, noon and evening, with the sky and the painted flagpole shadow different in each, then a winter noon and a summer noon. In each scene students put a marker stone on the tip of the shadow, then set the tower clock with a slider for hours and half-hours to match the sun. Wrong ideas to surprise: the shadow is longest at noon; the sun stays in one place; winter and summer days are the same length. Students order three shadow ropes from short to long, and read a three-bar chart of daylight hours for winter, spring and summer. Bag puzzles: an oil can for the stuck clock door, a ladder to reach the bell rope. Invent a small twist about a rooster that crows at the wrong time. Build scene: students place sun, shadow and label pieces to make their own sundial. Every line is very short and read aloud. Debrief: why is the noon shadow short?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Morning Square.** On-ramp. Walk, talk to Amara, pick up a marker stone, put it on the shadow tip. The clock reads 3:00 and the baker is cross.
2. **Bell Loft.** Oil the door, climb, ring the bell. A short cutscene sweeps the sun across the sky.
3. **Noon Square.** Predict first: will the shadow be longer or shorter? It is tiny. Mark it and set the clock to 12:00.
4. **Evening Square.** The shadow now points the other way and is long. Lamps come on. Set the clock to 6:30.
5. **Winter Noon and Summer Noon.** Two doors in the tower. Count the lit-lamp hours in each and fill the chart.
6. **Sundial Garden.** Build scene, then the debrief with Amara.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution" title="The twist">
The rooster crows at noon in winter because the morning was so dark. Students use the shadow, not the rooster, to tell the time.
</Aside>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Three shadow ropes lie tangled. Lay them side by side and order them. The shortest is the noon rope.
- The clock slider only rings true when the hands match the sun picture: sun at the top means 12:00.
- The wrong guess at noon is kept on the ground as a grey stone next to the real mark, so the surprise stays visible.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
In Summer Noon, find the hour when your own avatar's shadow is shortest.
</Aside>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

A real day takes a day and a real year takes a year, and first graders are in class at the same hours every day. They never see the morning, noon and evening shadows of one pole in a lesson, and never winter beside summer. A video can show a time-lapse, but the child has not put a stone where they think the shadow will land. Chalk shadows outside are excellent and should follow this mission, but they need sun on the right day and cannot show the seasons. Here every child predicts, sees the miss, and tries again in 35 minutes.

</BlueprintWhy>
