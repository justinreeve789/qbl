```text wrap title="The Canyon That Kept a Diary: generator prompt"
Make a mission called The Canyon That Kept a Diary for grade 4. Teach and assess NGSS 4-ESS1-1, 3-LS4-1 and 4-ESS2-1 and Common Core RI.4.7. Students are junior geologists working with Dr. Noor, who says the canyon keeps a diary and each rock layer is a page. Scenes, joined by ladders going down: Rim Camp up high; Red Ledge, a sandstone layer with ripple marks and small reptile tracks; Shell Wall, a pale limestone layer packed with seashells and coral; Fern Hollow, a dark shale overhang with fern and fish fossils; and River Floor with a flowing river. Before the climb ask for a prediction: where is the oldest page, top or bottom? In each layer students use a brush on fossil spots to collect a rubbing, and a character asks what kind of place leaves that behind. On River Floor an instrument has a slider for water flow, a switch for hard or soft rock and a slider for years from 100 to 1,000,000; a meter shows how deep the river cuts. Wrong ideas to surprise: the top layer is the oldest; the canyon cracked open in one day; seashells cannot be found far from the sea. Bag puzzles: a rope and a hook combine to reach a ledge; a lantern lights the overhang. Invent a small twist where rain upstream turns the river brown and students catch a jar of it and watch it settle into layers. Include a cross-section diagram and a time ribbon to read. Build scene at Rim Camp: students place the rubbings in layer order on a diary wall and label each past landscape. Debrief: how can a sea leave shells on a dry cliff?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Rim Camp.** On-ramp. Take the brush, make the top-or-bottom prediction, start down.
2. **Red Ledge.** Ripples in stone and three-toed tracks. Students see dunes before anyone says desert.
3. **Shell Wall.** The surprise. Seashells in a cliff with no sea for a thousand miles.
4. **Fern Hollow.** Lantern needed. Ferns and a fish pressed in black rock.
5. **River Floor.** The instrument. A hundred years barely scratches the rock; a million years cuts the canyon.
6. **Brown water.** The twist. The jar settles into bands while students watch.
7. **Rim Camp again.** Build the diary wall, then the debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Three rubbings, three landscapes. A wrong match gets a reason: “Ferns need wet ground. Would they grow on a dune?”
- Ordering the pages: students who said the top is oldest see their guess beside the cross-section and try again.
- The river goal is a cut 300 metres deep. No flow setting gets there in 100 years, which is the point.
- The jar: which band settled first? It links the river back to the layers above.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
Find the hidden track that crosses from one layer into the next and explain why it cannot.
</Aside>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

The two obstacles in this topic are a place no class can visit and a span of time no class can watch. Here going down is going back in time, and students find the shells themselves instead of being told about them. A sand jar models layering well and a stream table models erosion well, and both belong in the unit, but neither holds fossils in place or lets a student dial up a million years. A canyon video is striking, yet it narrates the answer before the student has guessed.

</BlueprintWhy>
