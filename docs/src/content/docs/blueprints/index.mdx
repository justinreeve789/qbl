A **blueprint** is a mission worked out on paper before anything is drawn. Each one starts from the standards a teacher has to cover, picks the ones that belong together, and builds one story that teaches them all. Every blueprint was chosen because the mission does something a classroom cannot: squeeze a year into a lesson, visit a canyon floor, or let a bridge fall down for free.

## How the set was chosen

A mission made the list only if it passed four tests.

1. **The standards are widely shared.** Most jurisdictions teach them, and teachers report them as hard to teach.
2. **The standards belong together.** One story covers them all, with no quiz bolted on.
3. **The mission adds something no other method can.** Compressed time, unreachable places, free failure, changed rules of nature, or teammates who truly need each other. [Why a mission, and when not](/docs/blueprints/why-questlab/) names all seven.
4. **QuestLab can build it today.** Parts that need hand work in the editor are listed in [Before you build](/docs/blueprints/build-notes/).

## What every blueprint has in common

All 24 follow the [game design rules](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/). The pages only point out what is special to each mission, so here is the spine they share.

- **A safe first scene** with something to click inside two minutes ([rule 2](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#2-make-the-first-scene-a-safe-on-ramp)).
- **A named role** with a profession's way of checking evidence: geologist, archivist, lake manager ([rule 9](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#9-give-students-a-role)).
- **Doing before naming.** Students use an idea before a character names it ([rule 25](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#25-explore-before-you-explain)).
- **A surprise for each wrong idea.** Students predict, see the prediction fail, then rebuild the idea with a character who asks questions ([rule 26](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#26-let-a-surprise-change-their-minds)).
- **A place to try things** at no cost, where a miss says why ([rules 10](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#10-give-them-a-place-to-experiment) and [11](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#11-give-feedback-right-away-and-say-why)).
- **Hints from characters** in three steps: a nudge, a pointer, a worked example ([rule 13](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#13-layer-the-help-and-let-characters-give-it)).
- **A shared goal, a payoff and one optional harder objective** ([rules 14](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#14-let-students-choose-their-challenge) and [15](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#15-give-the-group-a-goal-and-a-payoff)).
- **Clues spread across scenes** so teammates need each other, with no fixed crew roles ([rule 16](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#16-make-teammates-need-each-other)).
- **One build scene near the end**, where students make something that uses the idea ([rule 24](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#24-let-students-make)).
- **A debrief** with a why question and a link to real life ([rule 19](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#19-end-with-an-after-action-review)).
- **Words one grade below the target grade**, and never above grade 9. The curriculum stays at the target grade.
