In the clinic an instrument called the survey has a chooser for how to pick the 20: the houses nearest the clinic; only houses that sent for a doctor; every tenth house on the list; or a spin of the wheel for each. Wrong ideas to surprise: any 20 houses are as good as any other 20; cases near the smell prove the smell is the cause; cases that do not fit ruin the pattern. Two cases seem not to fit: the brewery workers on the lane stay well because the brewery has its own well, and a widow far up the hill is sick because her son carries her water from the lane pump. Invent a twist around one of them. Final puzzle: persuade the alderman with the strongest evidence, fetch a wrench from the smith and take the handle off the pump. Payoff: the case chart falls. Build scene: students place and label a public notice. Debrief: why did it matter which doors you knocked on?

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Clinic.** On-ramp. A blank map and a full waiting room. Dr. Mensah hands over the first household card.
2. **Brackwater Lane.** Knock on doors. The dot map fills in at the clinic as the team works.
3. **Market Square.** Two pumps, one smell. The alderman explains bad air, convincingly.
4. **The survey.** The instrument. Two of the four sampling plans cannot answer the question at all.
5. **Brewery Yard and Hillside Cottage.** The cases that do not fit, until students ask where each gets its water.
6. **Council Chamber.** Choose the evidence. Win the order. Take off the handle.
7. **Weeks later.** The chart falls. Build the notice and debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Sampling: houses nearest the clinic all use the lane pump, so there is nothing to compare them with. The miss says so.
- The table: 54 of 90 is 60 percent; 6 of 110 is about 5 percent. Students state the association in words.
- Smell test: households beside the tannery that use the square pump are mostly well.
- The two odd cases turn from problems into the strongest evidence of all.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
Rerun the wheel sample five times, record the five estimates, and describe how much they vary.
</Aside>

## Teacher plan

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

A statistics worksheet arrives with the data already collected, which removes the hardest and most important decision: whom to ask. A real class survey restores that decision but has low stakes and a tiny population. Here there are 200 households, only 20 visits, and a town depending on the answer, so a biased sample has a visible cost and can be rerun to compare. Evidence is spread along a street, so the team must pool it. A lecture can define a representative sample in one sentence, and students can repeat the sentence without ever having been misled by a bad one.

</BlueprintWhy>
