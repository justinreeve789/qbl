## Standards and evidence

<BlueprintStandards />

**Other curricula.** Also fits HSF-IF.B.4 (reading the titration curve), a lighter grade 8 run under MS-PS1-2, and Ontario Grade 11 chemistry. These are matches by topic, not checked codes.

<Aside type="note" title="Good to know">
Worked numbers: a 25.0 mL sample of 0.000500 mol/L sulfuric acid needs 25.0 mL of 0.00100 mol/L sodium hydroxide. A 200,000 cubic metre lake then holds 100,000 mol of acid and needs 10 tonnes of limestone. A chemistry teacher should check these before publishing.
</Aside>

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Lakeshore.** On-ramp. Three test strips, three colours. The worst is by the orange inflow.
2. **Mine Tunnel.** Follow the orange stream to its source. Collect a sample of the drainage itself.
3. **Field Lab Tent.** Titrate. Overshoot. Start again. Watch the curve stand almost still, then jump.
4. **The sums.** Sample to litre to lake. The two-for-one ratio is where most first answers go wrong.
5. **Village Hall.** Present the dose. The treasurer asks what happens if it is doubled by mistake.
6. **One Year Later.** The twist. Fish near the far shore, strips turning red again by the inflow.
7. **A better plan.** Compare options, place the monitoring points, debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Endpoint: the colour holds for 24 mL and flips inside 1 mL. Students find it by slowing down, which the graph teaches.
- pH 2.6 against pH 3.6: how many times more acid? Ten, not a little.
- The ratio: two sodium hydroxide for each sulfuric acid. The one-to-one answer is accepted as an answer, then tested and seen to overshoot.
- Units: 0.000500 mol in each litre, 1,000 litres in each cubic metre, 200,000 cubic metres.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
The tunnel stream delivers 40 mol of acid a day. How much limestone a year does a channel need?
</Aside>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

Handling a burette is a physical skill and must be learned at a real bench. What goes wrong in that first real lab, though, is conceptual: students overshoot because they do not know the curve is flat and then steep, and each retry costs ten minutes and fresh reagent. Here ten overshoots take ten minutes and the curve draws itself as they go. No school lab can then scale the flask up to a lake, let a wrong mole ratio play out as an overdose, or come back a year later. A lecture can show the sums and a video can show the colour change; neither lets the student be responsible for the dose.

</BlueprintWhy>
