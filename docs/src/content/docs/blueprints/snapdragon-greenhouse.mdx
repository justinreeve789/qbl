```text wrap title="The Snapdragon Greenhouse: generator prompt"
Make a mission called The Snapdragon Greenhouse for grades 7 to 8. Teach and assess NGSS MS-LS3-2 and Common Core 7.SP.C.6, 7.SP.C.7 and 7.SP.C.8. Students are plant breeders working for Head Grower Mateus at a seed company that must deliver 100 pink snapdragons for the spring parade. Scenes: a potting bench, a crossing house, a seedling room, a chance bench, a clone row and the parade float. Snapdragon colour is real incomplete dominance: red plants carry two red factors, white plants carry two white factors, and pink plants carry one of each. Do not explain this until students have seen the results. First students take a cutting from a red plant and grow an identical red plant. Then they use a pollen brush on a red flower and a white flower and grow the seeds: every plant is pink. Ask for a prediction: what will pink crossed with pink give? Then show it. At the chance bench an instrument has a chooser for each parent and a wheel that spins once per parent for every seed to pick which factor is passed on; a Sow 8 seeds button adds to counters for red, pink and white, and a shared team tally adds up everyone's seeds. Students fill a two-by-two table of what each parent can pass on. Wrong ideas to surprise: colours blend like paint, so pink parents give only pink; eight seeds will split exactly 2, 4 and 2; a result that misses the model means the model is wrong. Ask which cross reaches 100 pinks fastest. Invent a twist where a fungus wipes out the clone row, because every clone is the same, while the seed-grown bench has survivors. Build scene at the float: students place flowers and label a display that shows the cross. Debrief: why did the team tally match the table better than your own tray?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Potting Bench.** On-ramp. Snip, pot, grow. The cutting is a copy of its parent.
2. **Crossing House.** Brush pollen from red to white. Collect the seed pod.
3. **Seedling Room.** All pink. Then the prediction, then the pink by pink tray: red, pink and white together.
4. **Chance Bench.** Wheel, counters, the two-by-two table, the team tally.
5. **Clone Row.** The twist. Row after row of identical plants, wilted. One bench of seed-grown plants has gaps and survivors.
6. **Parade Float.** Payoff. A hundred pinks in bloom. Build and debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- The table: each pink parent can pass red or white. Four boxes, one red, two pink, one white.
- A tray of eight that gives 1, 6 and 1 is not broken. Students choose the best explanation from four.
- Fastest route to 100 pinks: red by white gives all pink; pink by pink gives about half.
- The fungus: why did every cutting die and only some seedlings?

</div>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

A real snapdragon generation takes months, so no class sees the second cross. The two ideas students resist, that the red was hidden rather than lost and that chance settles down only in large numbers, both need many fast trials and a shared count. Coin-flip genetics on paper is a fair classroom rival, but pooling thirty students' tallies by hand takes most of the lesson and the coins are not flowers. Here the wheel is the chance, the tray is the result, and the class total builds itself. A lecture can draw the table; it cannot make a student distrust it, test it and come back convinced.

</BlueprintWhy>
