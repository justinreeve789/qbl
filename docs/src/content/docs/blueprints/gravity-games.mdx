```text wrap title="Gravity Games: generator prompt"
Make a mission called Gravity Games for grades 6 to 8. Teach and assess NGSS MS-PS2-4 and MS-PS3-2 and Common Core 6.RP.A.3 and 6.EE.C.9. Students are sports engineers working with Coach Ilse, a physicist, to write one fair rulebook for skyball, a school ball game played on four worlds. Scenes: a starship gym at Earth gravity, a shuttle bay with portals, a Moon dome, a Mars court, a dome on the invented heavy world Brakka, and a rulebook room. Use these values for gravity in newtons per kilogram: Moon 1.6, Mars 3.7, Earth 9.8, Brakka 19.6 (Brakka is the size of Earth with twice the mass). The domes have no air. In each world an instrument called the drop rig has a chooser for the ball (rubber, steel or foam, with different masses), a slider for drop height up to 3 metres, and a Drop button; meters show fall time, bounce height and landing energy. Each world also has a spring scale that reads weight in newtons and a balance beam that compares mass. Students explore freely first and data from all four worlds is needed for the rulebook, so teams split up. Ask for predictions. Wrong ideas to surprise: the heavy ball lands first; there is no gravity on the Moon; things have less mass on the Moon; a dropped ball bounces higher on the Moon (it bounces to the same height, more slowly). A wall table of mass against weight on each world gives the unit rate and the equation weight = g x mass. Invent a twist where the old rule says the ball must weigh 6 newtons, which is a different ball on every world, so students rewrite the rule in kilograms. Final task: choose hoop heights so a jump shot is equally hard everywhere. Build scene on the Mars court: students place a hoop and lines and label their reasons. Debrief: what stays the same on every world, and what changes?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Ship Gym.** On-ramp. Drop a ball, weigh it, balance it. Earth numbers go on the first row of the table.
2. **Shuttle Bay.** Three portals. Teams choose who goes where.
3. **Moon Dome, Mars Court, Brakka Dome.** Same rig, same balls. Slow falls on the Moon, hard landings on Brakka.
4. **The 6 newton rule.** The twist. Each world's referee holds up a different ball and all of them weigh 6 N.
5. **Rulebook Room.** The table, the four lines on one graph, the hoop heights.
6. **Mars Court.** Build, first match, debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Steel and foam land together on every world. The fall time changes with the world, never with the ball.
- Bounce: from 2 m the rubber ball returns to the same height on the Moon as on Earth. Only the time differs.
- Scale against balance: the spring scale changes on every world, the balance beam never does. Which one measures mass?
- The table gives 1.6, 3.7, 9.8 and 19.6 newtons for each kilogram. Students predict the weight of a 5 kg kit bag on Mars.

</div>

<Aside type="tip" title="Optional harder objective">
A pendulum clock keeps the match time. Does it run true on the Moon? Test and explain.
</Aside>

## Teacher plan

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

Gravity is the one variable a classroom can never change. Every real drop happens at 9.8, so students cannot test their ideas about the Moon; they can only be told. Astronaut footage is the usual substitute and it helps, but it cannot be rerun with a different ball or a different height. Here students change the world itself and repeat the same fair test four times. Hands-on work still has a job: a real spring scale and balance give the feel of the two tools, and the plan starts with a real drop.

</BlueprintWhy>
