```text wrap title="One Sky, Two Views: generator prompt"
Make a mission called One Sky, Two Views for grades 6 to 8. Teach and assess NGSS MS-ESS1-1 (lunar phases, eclipses and seasons) and Common Core RST.6-8.7. Students are almanac makers working with Astronomer Leila at Lantern Rock Observatory, which must tell the island's fishing fleet when the nights will be bright and when the days will be long. Scenes: a ground deck outdoors at night, an orbit room, a season hall and an almanac desk. In the orbit room an instrument called the orrery shows the Sun, Earth and Moon from above; it has a slider that moves the Moon to eight positions around the Earth and a switch that makes the Moon's path flat or tilted by 5 degrees. It shows how much of the Moon's face is lit as seen from Earth and whether Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. The ground deck shows the Moon in the sky for the position the team has set, so one teammate moves the Moon while another reports what they see; show the shapes first and name the phases afterwards. In the season hall a second instrument has a slider for Earth's tilt from 0 to 30 degrees, a slider for Earth's distance from the Sun from 147 to 152 million kilometres and a chooser for the month; meters show the height of the noon Sun and the hours of daylight for a northern island and a southern island. Ask for predictions. Wrong ideas to surprise: phases are caused by Earth's shadow; the Moon between Earth and Sun looks full; summer comes when Earth is closest to the Sun (Earth is closest in early January); the Moon makes its own light. Invent a twist where fog hides the sky on the night the fleet sails, students predict the Moon from the model, and the fog clears to prove it. Build scene at the almanac desk: students place the eight Moon shapes in order and label where the Moon is for each. Debrief: why is there not an eclipse every month?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Ground Deck.** On-ramp. Look up. Tonight's Moon is a thin curve. Leila asks what it will look like in a week, and takes guesses.
2. **Orbit Room.** The orrery. Free play, then the relay: “Give me a half Moon, lit on the right.”
3. **Shadow test.** Students who blame Earth's shadow switch the path to flat and get an eclipse every single month.
4. **Season Hall.** Tilt to zero, slide the distance, and nothing happens to the daylight. Tilt back, and north and south swap seasons.
5. **Fog night.** The twist. Predict from the model, write it in the almanac, wait for the fog to lift.
6. **Almanac Desk.** Build the phase chart. The fleet sails under the Moon the students predicted. Debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- The relay: the deck observer describes a shape, and the orbit room teammate must find the position that makes it.
- Position zero, Moon between Earth and Sun. Predicted full by most; seen as nothing at all.
- Flat path against tilted path: count eclipses in twelve turns of the slider.
- Seasons: find a setting where the northern island has long days in June. Then try to do it with distance alone.

</div>

The almanac needs the date of the next full Moon after tonight's thin curve. Use the 29.5 day cycle.
</Aside>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

The shadow idea for phases and the distance idea for seasons are famously durable, and many adults still hold them after years of being told otherwise. A lamp and a ball in a dark room is the standard hands-on answer and should stay in the unit. Its limits are that each student holds one viewpoint at a time, and nobody can switch the tilt off or pull the Earth closer to see what changes. Here the space view and the ground view are joined live between teammates, and tilt and distance are separate sliders. A month passes in a minute, and the student's own wrong prediction is the thing being tested.

</BlueprintWhy>
