```text wrap title="The Word Forge: generator prompt"
Make a mission called The Word Forge for grades 4 to 5. Teach and assess Common Core L.4.4a, L.4.4b, L.5.4b and RF.4.3a. Students are apprentice wordsmiths working for Smith Odalys in a mountain forge where words become tools. Scenes: the forge floor, a stone quarry, a lookout ledge up high, a vault corridor underground and a showroom. Students collect eight carved root stones spread across the scenes, each with its meaning cut into it: tele (far), micro (small), scope (look at), phone (sound), graph (write or draw), therm (heat), meter (measure) and photo (light). Stones are never used up. Combining two stones forges a real tool that does what the parts mean: telescope, microscope, telephone, microphone, telegraph, photograph, thermometer, phonograph, telephoto, micrometer and telemeter. Each obstacle states a need in plain words and never names the tool: read a sign on a far tower; read tiny writing on a key; hear a whisper behind the vault door; check that the forge is hot enough; copy a map using light; send written words to the valley. Students use the right tool on each. A wrong tool gets a reason based on its meaning. Pin notes around the forge that use each word in a sentence as context clues. Wrong idea to surprise: long words must be memorised whole. Invent a twist where the labels burn off the tool shelf and students must pick tools by meaning alone. Optional harder objective: the vault needs something to measure a thing that is far away, a word students have never seen. Build scene in the showroom: students invent a new tool from two stones, place it and label its name and job. Debrief: what would a thermograph do, and how do you know?
```

## Scenario design

<Steps>

1. **Forge Floor.** On-ramp. Two stones lie by the anvil. Put them together and a telescope drops out. Nobody has explained anything yet.
2. **Quarry and Lookout Ledge.** Gather stones. Use the telescope on the far tower to read the first clue.
3. **Vault Corridor.** Tiny writing, a whisper, a locked door. Three needs, three tools.
4. **Fire trouble.** The twist. The fire sinks, the labels are ash, and the right tool must be chosen from five.
5. **The vault.** Bonus. “Measure what is far.” Students forge a telemeter before they know the word exists.
6. **Showroom.** Build an invented tool, then the debrief.

</Steps>

## Puzzles

<div class="bp-puzzles">

- Need to tool: “I must look at something far.” The stones say far and look at.
- Wrong tool, useful reason: “A microscope looks at small things. The tower is not small. It is far.”
- Context clues: “The smith held the \_\_\_ to the wall and heard the mice.” Students choose the tool that fits the sentence.
- Joke combinations still mean something. A phonograph plays a tune, and Kit asks what graph is doing in that word.

</div>

Forge all eleven tools and sort them by shared stone.
</Aside>

## Teacher plan

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Before" icon="comment">
    Write telescope and microscope. “What do they share? What might that part mean?”
  </Card>
  <Card title="During" icon="laptop">
    Solo or pairs. Listen for students saying the need aloud before they combine; prompt it if they are guessing.
  </Card>
  <Card title="After" icon="approve-check">
    Give three unseen words, such as photometer, and have pairs explain them. The report shows first-try rate on tool choice and who solved the bonus.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Example" icon="star">
    Run it at the start of a roots unit, then keep a class wall of root stones and add a word each time one turns up in science or reading.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## Why QuestLab is the best tool here

<BlueprintWhy>

In a list or on a worksheet, a root is a fact to memorise. With word-part cards, any two cards make a word and nothing happens next. Here meaning has a consequence: the tool works only if the student understood its parts, and the wrong tool fails for a reason they can read. That turns morphology into a strategy students use on purpose, which is what the standard asks. There is no real-materials rival for this one, and a lecture or video can only present the list.

</BlueprintWhy>
