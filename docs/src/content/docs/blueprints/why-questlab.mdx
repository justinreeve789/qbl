A mission is the best tool when the thing to be learned is too slow, too far, too big, too risky or too invisible to act on in a classroom. It is the wrong tool when the real material is cheap, safe and fast. Every [blueprint](/docs/blueprints/) ends by naming which of these seven advantages it leans on.

## Against the other ways to teach

The comparison is the same on every blueprint page, so it is set out once here.

| Method | Strong at | Falls short when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lecture** | Naming and organising an idea students already half hold | Students hold a wrong idea that feels right. Telling rarely moves it |
| **Video** | Showing places and events vividly | The student never commits to a prediction, so nothing is at stake when the answer arrives |
| **Hands-on in class** | The senses, real measuring, real materials | The thing takes weeks, costs money for every try, cannot be broken safely, or hides its cause |
| **A mission** | Acting on a system, failing cheaply, seeing what follows, needing teammates | The goal is a skill of the hands: using a ruler, a burette or a brush |

Hands-on work is the strongest rival. That is why every blueprint's teacher plan names a real activity to run beside the mission: a chalk shadow, a bean on the windowsill, a craft-stick bridge, a real titration.

<Aside type="caution" title="A caution on the word best">
The case made on each blueprint page is a design argument, not the result of a trial. The [game design rules](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#where-these-rules-come-from) rest on summaries of the research, and much of that research is case studies. Playtest each mission with your own students, and read the [reports](/docs/editor/reports/) before you trust it.
</Aside>

## How a mission changes a wrong idea

Most of the blueprints are built around an idea students bring with them that feels right and is wrong: a quarter is bigger than a third because four is more than three, heavy things fall faster, the top rock layer is the oldest. Being told the right answer seldom shifts ideas like these. Each blueprint uses the same three beats, from [How students learn](/docs/educators/how-students-learn/).

1. **Predict.** The student commits to an answer before anything happens. The mission records it.
2. **See it fail.** The world does what the world does. The student's own guess stays on screen beside the result.
3. **Rebuild.** A character asks questions until the student can say why. The character never simply says it.

A lecture can do the third beat and a video can do the second. Only something the student acts on can do all three, with their own prediction at stake.
