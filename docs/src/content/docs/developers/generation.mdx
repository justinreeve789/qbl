`php artisan qbl:generate` makes a whole mission from a teacher's prompt: it aligns the mission to curriculum standards, writes the story, scenes, characters, dialogue, questions and events, draws every picture in the house art style, and imports the result as a private draft for the teacher to review.

```sh
docker compose exec app php artisan qbl:generate \
  "Students find out why an orchard on an alien planet has flowers but no fruit" \
  --grade=3 --subject=Science --framework=NGSS --players=2-6 --owner=teacher@school.org
```

It prints its progress and ends with the new scenario's id. The mission is **private and unpublished**: open it in the editor, play it with "Test this scene", change anything, then publish and assign it.

Step | What | Kept in
---- | ---- | -------
1\. Align | Chooses 1-3 primary standards (and up to 3 supporting ones) from those imported, and writes measurable learning objectives, vocabulary and misconceptions to address | `scenario_generations.alignment`
2\. Design | Writes the **blueprint**: story, scenes and how they connect, characters, items, interactions, the checklist, the briefing and the final report. It is checked, and sent back to be corrected until it can be built | `scenario_generations.blueprint`, `blueprint.json`
3\. Draw | Draws every picture: each character's design, then their animations (from AutoSprite in one request per character when `AUTOSPRITE_API_KEY` is set, else drawn as strips; their portrait is cut from the design); the mission's small things, several to a picture; doors and holes; every background; the mission patch | `assets/` in the work folder, `scenario_generations.manifest`
3½\. Survey | Reads each finished background with a model that can see (the judge's): where the floor really meets the buildings, walls, fences and water, traced onto the painted line; the things standing out on the floor that people walk around, each lifted out of the picture pixel for pixel as an item with its sort line; and where the doors, ladders, wall things and wall lights it was asked to paint ended up. See [Reading the backgrounds](#reading-the-backgrounds) | `survey/` and `assets/cutout-*.png` in the work folder
4\. Build | Turns the blueprint into rows of the QuestLab tables, using the patterns from [Patterns](/docs/developers/extensions/patterns/#patterns). Gives each character a voice from what the blueprint says of them, a different one each | 
4½\. Voice | Records every line the characters say, with the time of every word, on the server's text-to-speech service ([Characters' voices](/docs/developers/voices/)). Free with Kokoro. If the service is down this is a warning, not a failure | `voices/` in the work folder
5\. Package | Compiles the rows into `scenario.json` and packs it with its pictures as an import ZIP, plus a preview of each scene | `scenario.zip`, `previews/`
6\. Import | Imports the ZIP exactly as the editor's "Import a ZIP" does, as a private draft owned by `--owner`; records its standards; publishes it if `--publish` | `scenarios` and the rest; `scenario_standards`

Those steps are the **classic** method's. The pipeline is pluggable: a *generation method* is a class that decides which steps run and which services do each job, and every run writes a trace of every step and AI call to its work folder. See [Generation methods and traces](/docs/developers/generation-methods/) to compare approaches or write your own.

Each run has a work folder (`QuestLab_GEN_WORK_DIR`, `/data/generations/{id}` in Docker). If a run stops (a service is down, a key runs out of credit) carry on where it stopped, without paying for anything twice:

```sh
php artisan qbl:generate --resume=12
```

The ZIP is an ordinary scenario archive: it can be imported again, on another installation or by hand (`php artisan qbl:generate --import=path/to/scenario.zip`).

## Options

Option | Effect
------ | ------
`--method=` | How to make it: `classic` (default, `QBL_GEN_METHOD`), `draft`, `gemini-judge`, `sprite-app`, `critic`, `best-of`, `outline-first`. `--list-methods` lists them. See [Generation methods](/docs/developers/generation-methods/).
`--redo=` | With `--resume`: do a step again (and every step after it), e.g. `--redo=design`.
`--grade=` | K, 1-12, or a range such as `6-8`. Only standards for that grade are offered.
`--subject=` | Helps choose standards and pitch the content.
`--framework=` | The standards set(s) to align to, as named when imported (repeatable).
`--standard=` | Exact standard codes to build the mission around, instead of choosing (repeatable). Each must be imported.
`--scenes=` | How many scenes. Default: the designer decides, usually 3 to 6.
`--players=2-6` | How many students play together. Team tasks never need more students than the minimum.
`--minutes=` | Rough playing time.
`--notes=` | Anything else for the designer ("set it in ancient Egypt", "no reading above grade 2").
`--format=` | `mission` (students play it) or `story` (it plays by itself, for students to watch; see [Stories](#stories)). Default: as the prompt reads, decided while aligning.
`--opening=` | How it starts: `action`, `balanced` (action first, story through discoveries) or `story`. Missions only. Default: chosen to suit the prompt while aligning. See [Game design rules: in the AI generator](/docs/educators/game-design-rules/#in-the-ai-generator).
`--focus=` | Rigor or play: `play`, `balanced` (quick hook, rigorous middle) or `rigor`. Default: chosen to suit the prompt.
`--freedom=` | Student freedom: `guided`, `balanced` (some choice of order) or `open`. Missions only. Default: chosen to suit the prompt.
`--owner=` | The teacher who owns the draft.
`--style=` | The art style (a folder in `resources/art-styles`). Default `QuestLab_GEN_STYLE`, `qbl-adventure`.
`--images=` | `gemini`, `openai`, `scenario` or `placeholder`.
`--animations=` | Who animates the characters: `autosprite` (the default when `AUTOSPRITE_API_KEY` is set; see [Animations from AutoSprite](#animations-from-autosprite)) or `draw` (strips drawn by the image service).
`--design-only` | Stop after the blueprint, to review the plan cheaply before any picture is drawn. Then `--resume`.
`--no-import` | Make the ZIP but do not import it.
`--publish` | Publish the imported draft.
`--no-judge` | Do not check pictures against the style (cheaper, less consistent).
`--no-voices` | Do not record the characters' lines; the browser reads them aloud instead.
`--attempts=` | Attempts per picture before the best is kept (default 3).
`--budget=` | Most to spend on AI services for this run, in US dollars (default `QuestLab_GEN_BUDGET_USD`, 5; `0` for none). See [What it costs](#what-it-costs).

## What it costs

A run reports what it spends on AI services, priced from list prices (`Spend`): after the design it says how many pictures drawing the mission will take and roughly what they will cost (so `--design-only` is a cheap way to see the price first), and at the end what it spent, split into pictures and language model. The running total is kept in the work folder (`spend.json`), so a resumed run carries on counting, and goes in the ZIP's README. The figures are estimates, not a bill.

Pictures are most of the cost. How it is kept down:

- **Small things several to a picture.** A mission's items, the tables and rocks small things stand on, and the poses of its creatures are drawn up to six to a picture, in a grid that is cut apart (`QuestLab_GEN_PROP_SHEETS`); the grid is judged once. A thing whose cell came out empty or spilled into the next is drawn on its own.
- **A cheaper model for small cut-outs.** Things, sheets of things, doors and holes and the badge end up a few hundred pixels across: with Gemini they go to `QuestLab_GEN_GEMINI_PROPS_MODEL` (default `gemini-3.1-flash-image`) at `QuestLab_GEN_GEMINI_PROPS_SIZE` (1K, or 2K for a sheet with big things), about half the price of Gemini 3 Pro Image. Backgrounds, characters and their animations stay on `QuestLab_GEN_GEMINI_MODEL`, and so do style anchors. Every picture is still judged.
- **Animations from AutoSprite** (when `AUTOSPRITE_API_KEY` is set): every character gets all ten animations in one request, about $0.95 at turbo (5 credits each at the Pro plan's ~$0.019 a credit), priced as `autosprite@turbo` in `Spend::PRICES`. That is more than drawing only the animations the mission plays, but it buys the full set once, so a teacher has jump, dance, talk and the rest to hand. A six-character mission adds about $6, so raise `QBL_GEN_BUDGET_USD` (or trim the list with `QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_ANIMATIONS`). The run checks the budget before each character's request.
- **Only the animations the mission plays** (when strips are drawn). Every character walks; a gesture is drawn only if it is seen: `wave` for a character students talk to, `cheer` for the guide at the end, and any a cutscene asks of them. Their standing picture is their idle (`QuestLab_GEN_IDLE_FRAMES=true` draws a breathing idle as well).
- **Portraits cut from the character sheet** (`QuestLab_GEN_PORTRAITS=crop`): the sheet is drawn large, so its head and shoulders make a sharp portrait that is exactly the character's design. `draw` draws one more picture per character instead.
- **A budget** (`QuestLab_GEN_BUDGET_USD`, `--budget=`). Once a run has spent it, no picture is redrawn for a better score (the best attempt is kept and flagged, as after the last attempt) and gestures are left out (the built-in moves stand in). Everything the mission needs is still made. At twice the budget the run stops; resume it with a higher one.

The language model costs less, and is kept down too:

- **Corrections as changes.** When the blueprint has problems, the model answers with only the changes that fix them (set this field, remove that one, add to a list) rather than writing the whole blueprint again, which cost as much as writing it the first time. Only if three rounds of changes do not fix it is it rewritten once.
- **Prompt caching.** The instructions are cached on every call, the brief across the designer's corrections, and the style anchors across the judge's calls, so repeated calls read them at a twentieth of the price.
- **A smaller model where it is enough.** Aligning to standards uses `QuestLab_GEN_ALIGN_MODEL` (default Sonnet); the judge uses `QuestLab_GEN_JUDGE_MODEL` (Sonnet). The mission itself is still written by `QuestLab_GEN_LLM_MODEL` (Opus).

For a mission like the test one (five scenes, two characters, eleven small things, three doors and holes), drawing once takes 17 pictures, about $1.95 at list prices, where it took 31, about $4.27, before; redraws come on top, up to the budget. Prices change: `QuestLab_GEN_PRICES` overrides any of them (JSON, the shape of `Spend::PRICES`).

## The audit log

`spend.json` is a running total. For auditing, every run also writes **one row per thing it did that took time or money** to the `generation_events` table (`GenerationEvents`; the migration is `2026_11_21_000100_create_generation_events_table`, and `php artisan migrate` creates it). The rows go in as it works, so a run that fails or is cancelled leaves its log up to that point, and a resumed run carries on numbering (`seq`) where it left off.

| Column | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `generation_id`, `seq`, `stage` | The run, the order things happened in, and the stage (aligning, designing, drawing, building, voicing, importing). |
| `type` | `llm` (a language-model call, the judge's too), `picture`, `voice` (a recorded line), `stage` (the clock on the wall for a stage), `estimate` (what drawing was expected to cost, to compare with what it did). |
| `kind`, `step`, `label`, `attempt` | What it was for. For a call: its purpose (`curriculum_alignment`, `mission_blueprint`, `style_review`...). For a picture: what was drawn (`background`, `sheet`, `strip`...). `step` is the picture's key (`strip:keeper:walk`); a judge call carries the key and attempt of the picture it judged. |
| `model`, `size`, `references` | The model, a picture's size, how many reference pictures were sent. |
| `started_at`, `duration_ms` | When it began (UTC, to the millisecond) and how long it took. |
| `input_tokens`, `output_tokens`, `cache_read_tokens`, `cache_write_tokens` | A call's tokens. Output includes the model's thinking. |
| `usd` | What it cost at list prices (see `Spend::PRICES`): an estimate, not a bill. A picture or call that failed is logged with the error and what was charged. |
| `score`, `outcome`, `note` | The judge's score for a picture attempt, and how it ended: `ok`, `error`, `truncated` (cut off by `max_tokens`), `refused`, `kept`, `discarded` (a better attempt was kept), `rejected` (a problem found before judging), `cancelled`, `failed`. |

Read it with `php artisan qbl:generation-log` (recent runs, with time and cost), `qbl:generation-log 42` (one run by stage and by kind of work), `--steps` (every event in order, `--type=llm` to filter) or `--json`. Administrators also get it from the editor's API: `GET /api/editor/generations/{id}/events`. Teachers do not see costs. A few queries:

```sql
-- where a run's money went
SELECT type, kind, COUNT(*) AS calls, SUM(duration_ms) / 1000 AS seconds, SUM(usd) AS usd
FROM generation_events WHERE generation_id = 42 AND type IN ('llm', 'picture', 'voice') GROUP BY type, kind ORDER BY usd DESC;

-- what the judge costs, across runs
SELECT generation_id, COUNT(*) AS calls, SUM(usd) AS usd FROM generation_events WHERE type = 'llm' AND kind = 'style_review' GROUP BY generation_id;

-- pictures that took several attempts
SELECT step, COUNT(*) AS attempts, SUM(usd) AS usd FROM generation_events WHERE type = 'picture' GROUP BY generation_id, step HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;
```

Logging never stops a run: a row that cannot be written is dropped (and noted in the PHP error log). Calls made outside a generation (voicing a scenario from the editor, say) are not in it.

## What a generated mission contains

The model writes a **blueprint**, never event ids or action lists. MissionBuilder turns it into rows with fixed patterns, so the result always passes the validator and plays:

Interaction | Built as
----------- | --------
talk | The character's click runs a conversation in a story box with their portrait (freeze, lines, unfreeze). A character with several conversations has the next that applies; with none left, a short reminder in a bubble.
collect | A click hotspot showing the item, which disappears once taken, then an infobox with what the student observes.
inspect | A click hotspot that opens an infobox; it can be looked at again. A `quiet` one has no question mark, only its name near it, like a detail: for something that looks like nothing much but turns out to matter.
question | A non-dismissible infobox with a button per answer. Every answer is explained; a wrong one invites another try. It can pop up by itself after another interaction (`after`). A **prediction** (`purpose: predict`) accepts every answer and records it, before students find out. A **debrief question** (`at_report`) is asked by the guide in the final report.
experiment | A hands-on **instrument** (`instrument`): a large box with its controls side by side on one panel, made for the scene. Sliders set a number (no typing), switches turn something on or off, a wheel of chance is spun; meters show a slider's value as a bar. What it shows changes the moment a control moves (`shows`: "💡 the bulb glows dimly"). Its test button tries it: with the `goal`'s settings it is done and the success is explained; otherwise the first `misses` entry whose settings fit says why not (or a general nudge), and the box opens again as it was left. Each control is saved in `c_<experiment>_<control>`. An older blueprint's single value moved up and down to a target becomes an instrument with one slider.
use | A thing from the **bag** used on something in the scene (`uses`, at `x`, on a drawn `item` or on something painted into the background, `look`), or given to a character (`character`). A hotspot that takes the thing (it tests `QL_Holding`): the student walks over, the thing leaves the bag unless it `keeps`, what happens is said or shown, and anything it `gives` goes into the bag. Clicked without the thing, or with the wrong one, it says what is needed (`hint`); a character who wants something turns down anything else and says what they need.
team_task | A shared (`room`) counter each student adds to once, shown to everyone in the scene: several students must each do their part.

Around the interactions:

- **Ways between scenes, not only at the edges.** A scene's exit is either an edge (walk off it; a top exit is a doorway at the centre of the back) or a **passage standing in the scene** where the blueprint puts it: a `door` into a building, a `gate`, a `cave` mouth, a `portal`, a `tunnel`, a `ladder` or `stairs_up` to somewhere higher, or a `hole`, `hatch` or `stairs_down` in the floor. Clicked, the student walks over, (hops into a hole,) fades out and arrives at the other end. Every connection is listed once and is two-way; the way back is added: out of a building towards the viewer (its bottom edge), back up a ladder out of a hole, down a hatch from a ladder, unless the blueprint says otherwise (`back`). Passages are drawn as cut-outs and stood in the scene, so they are always where the hotspot is; the background is asked to leave room for them. `Connections` works all of this out for the validator, the art and the builder alike.
- **Scenes of different kinds**: `outdoor`, `indoor`, `underground`, `up_high`, `underwater`, which the background prompt describes.
- **Things at their real size, in sensible places.** Each item has `size_cm`, its real longest side, and is drawn to scale beside students 130 cm tall (`Sizes`): a 12 cm vial is about 62 pixels, a crate is waist-high, a door is taller than a person (small things are drawn a little larger than true so they can still be seen and clicked). Where an interaction's item goes (`placement`): `ground` (out on the floor, at varied depths, so students can walk behind it), `back` (where the floor meets the scenery), `surface` (small things, on a table indoors or a flat rock outside, which is drawn and placed for them and is solid) or `wall` (hung on the back wall, only in indoor and underground scenes). Without a placement, or with one that does not suit the thing's size or the scene, it goes by size: small things on a surface, middling ones on the floor, big ones at the back.
- **Backgrounds at the same scale as the people in them.** An image model left to itself paints scenery at any size, so a house can dwarf the students or a doorway come up to their knees. Every background prompt says how tall a 130 cm child is at the back and the front of the floor, as a share of the picture's height, and how big a doorway, a table and a grown-up are beside them (`AssetDirector::scaleNotes`), and sends a **scale guide** with it: a grey picture of the floor with child-sized figures and a doorway outline, at the shape the background is drawn (`ImageTools::scaleGuide`; `QBL_GEN_SCALE_GUIDE=false` leaves it out). The style judge is told to mark down scenery out of scale with the people.
- **A point-and-click adventure.** What students `collect` goes into their **bag** (a bar of things at the foot of the screen); an interaction can hand something over too (`gives`: the keeper gives a key, the chest holds a map). Things are used on the scene or given to characters (`use`, above), and two can be put together to make a third (`combinations`: a hook and some twine make a hook on a string), which the checklist can count. The validator plays the mission through with the bag: nothing is used before something puts it in the bag, and nothing is used up before the last thing that needs it.
- **Cutscenes.** After an interaction, a twist or the finale, a little scene can play for everyone there (`cutscene`): characters, or the student, walk somewhere, say a line in a bubble, make a gesture (drawn if the character has it, else a move every figure can make: jump, spin, dance, wave), wait, leave for good (walking off an edge), or **fall** (slip out of sight, crying out: off a cliff, into a crevasse). A shared `away_<character>` hides someone who has left or fallen. The guide never leaves or falls, and nobody leaves while students still need them.
- **Arrivals and rescues.** The first time anyone comes into a scene, something can happen for everyone there (`arrival`: a title, lines, a cutscene), once (a shared `arrival_<scene>`). Use it for a tense moment: the robot rolls over to say hello and slips off the edge of the terrace. Whoever falls is out of reach until an interaction that `rescues` them is done in that scene (a use on the spot, an instrument, a team task or an inspect; never a gift to them, since they cannot be reached): it shows only while they are in trouble (its item is best a picture of them hanging on), and once anyone has done it they fade back in where they stood, for everyone (a shared `saved_<rescue>`, which is what the checklist reads). Talks with them and things given to them wait for the rescue (tidy adds it to their `requires`), and someone who falls stands still until then. The validator checks that everyone who falls has a rescue, and that nothing needs them while they are away.
- **Every thing in the bag is used.** What goes into the bag is used on something, given to someone, or put together into something that is; anything else is an error ("make it an inspect instead"). Red herrings are only to look at.
- **Details.** Up to five things per scene that are only to look at (`details`): painted into the background, named when a student is near or points at them, and clicked, what the student notices in a bubble. Colour, jokes, red herrings; never collected, never counted.
- **Living skies, lights and the air.** An outdoor or up-high scene can have a living `sky` (a time of day, and what it slowly becomes over ten minutes: a sunset from `golden` until `dusk`), with `clouds` (`none`, `few`, `many`). Its background, and its far view if it has one, are painted with a flat chroma sky that is cut out, so the game's sky shows behind them; one band of white clouds is drawn for the whole mission (`clouds`, 2400 pixels wide, empty at both ends so it repeats) and drifts across, tinted with the sky's light, a second lower band for `many`. `lights` (up to four) are things drawn and stood in the scene, lit (`lantern`, `torch`, `candle`, `campfire`, `lamp`, `beacon`, `crystal`, `screen`, each with its own colour, reach, height and way of moving); the glow is put on the brightest part of the picture (`ImageTools::brightSpot`), or where such a light usually is, at the size the thing is drawn there. A campfire is in the way; a lantern is not. A light can switch with the story: `on_after` names an interaction, combination, step or twist, and the light comes on once it is done (or has happened); `off_after` names one after which it goes out (not a step of several interactions, which has no single opposite). They become the light's `conditions`, read live like a passage's `requires`, and the thing that holds the light stays where it is. Stories have neither. `atmosphere` (up to two: fireflies, leaves, snow, rain, dust, bubbles) goes straight into the scene. The validator keeps them to places that make sense: no sky indoors, no rain in a greenhouse, bubbles only under water, nothing hung on a wall outdoors.
- **Rich observations.** What students observe (`info`) can add a coloured note (`callout`: tip, fact, warn, think), flip cards (`cards`), a card that opens (`reveal`) and a large `picture` of one of the mission's items; a box with any of these is large.
- **No crew roles.** Every student can do everything; a teacher who wants to split the work does it in class. (Blueprints with `roles`, `role` or `share` from before are tidied: those are dropped.)
- **Scenes that feel alive.** Creatures come and go now and then (`ambient`: a bird lands, hops about and flies off; moths flit by; a beetle crosses the floor), each a little sprite sheet of its poses drawn with the props. Characters can stroll between spots in their scene (`walks`) and say lines to themselves (`chatter`), and extra characters who are not needed for the plot give a place its colour. A fun thing to look at needs no object: its `look` is painted into the background, with a question mark over it (or none, for a detail).
- **A scene to build in** (`build`, in at most one scene: the constructivist rule *Let students make*). `prompt` (what to make, at most 300 characters), `tools` (`place`, `label`, `picture`, `hotspot`) and `pieces` (item keys, for `place`). The builder writes it as the scene's [student building](/docs/editor/student-building/) settings (`params.building`, with the items' pictures as the pieces, approval on). `place` without a piece that has a picture is dropped. Nothing can wait on a build.
- **Depth.** A scene can be `wide` (3600 pixels instead of 2250, drawn 21:9 and kept from the bottom) with a far view behind it (`distance`): the background is asked for a flat chroma sky, which is cut out, and the far view is drawn on its own and drifts slower than the scene (parallax) as students walk. Something close to the viewer can stand in front (`foreground`: a strip along the bottom, such as the top of a wall, or a piece in a corner), and students walk behind it; a strip along the bottom lets them come until their legs are hidden, no further. Figures and things are drawn a little smaller at the back of the floor than at its front (a scale area, 90% to 108%), and the floor can end at walls or a drop (`floor.left`, `floor.right`) and have things lying in it that students walk around (`floor.blocked`, painted in and blocked off).
- **Quiet marks.** A thing to click shows a small question mark (drawn in code, the same in every mission, not by the image model) and its name only while a student is near it or the pointer is over it (`show_near`). Nothing pulses: every label the generator makes says `pulse: false`.
- **Twists and turns, and their pace.** A twist makes things harder; a turn changes luck or a heart. For each mission, six ideas are picked at random from `Design/TwistIdeas.php` (26 of them: swapped labels, a confident mistake, a power cut, the tide turning, a collector animal, fog, a misread map, a code, a hollow echo, an unexpected ally, a lucky gust...) and shown in the brief to show the range, and at least one twist must be **invented** for the mission, out of its place, story and topic (`invented: true`; the validator refuses one copied from the ideas). **Big tense moments** (someone falls, is trapped or swept away, and students save them) are kept rare: whether a mission may have one is decided by chance before it is designed (`QBL_GEN_BIG_MOMENT_CHANCE`, 0.25 by default) and written into the blueprint (`pace.big_moment`); without it, a fall is an error, and with it, there is at most one.
- **Twists.** A surprise right after an interaction or step (`after`): flashed across the screen of everyone in the scene, with a startled hop and the characters' reactions. It can close a way out of that scene (`blocks`) and put a solid obstacle in front of it (`obstacle`) until an interaction that `resolves` it is done; students elsewhere hear about it when they next arrive somewhere. The far side of a blocked way stays open, so nobody is ever shut out.
- **Hidden discoveries** (`hidden`) appear only when a student walks close.
- **Characters at their size, that move.** Every character is drawn standing (idle), walking and as a portrait, as tall as `height_cm` says beside 130 cm students: a grown-up is taller than the students, a small robot comes up to their waist. One that `walks` strolls between its spots (a routine run by the game server, seen by everyone); an older blueprint's `wanders` paces either side of its spot; `chatter` lines are said in a bubble now and then.
- **The debrief.** Once every required step is done, the guide's label changes to the report. The guide asks the debrief questions not yet answered right, and only then celebrates and completes the mission, showing how many questions the student got right first time. With an answer still wrong, the student is sent to think again and comes back.

Also built: exits that stay closed until something is done (with a message), spawn points for arriving through every way in, the guide's briefing on first arrival, the checklist (with counts), awards, and a mission patch in the marketplace.

The validator also checks the new parts: passages have a place and a look and keep apart, two scenes connect once, there are one to three debrief questions and none is part of a step, every twist that blocks a way has something that sets it right, reachable without going through that way, what the bag holds comes from somewhere and lasts as long as it is needed, cutscenes only use characters who are there, an instrument's goal can be reached with its controls' ranges and options (and is not met before students change anything) and what it shows and its misses read controls it has, everyone who falls is rescued, every thing in the bag is used, a far view is only behind an outdoor scene, a foreground along the bottom never walls off a way out towards the viewer, the floor reaches every edge students leave by and is clear in front of passages, and only one scene has a build, with a task, known tools and pieces that are items.

The designer's brief (and the outline's and the critic's) also gets a **Thinking stage** line from the grade (`DesignRules::stage()`: Piaget's stages, for the youngest grade of a range), which the constructivist rules in `DesignRules::missionRules()` and `storyRules()` refer to.

**Variables** a teacher report can read: `done_<interaction or combination>` (1 once done; for a question, once answered correctly), `q_<question>_first` (the first answer chosen, 1-based), `q_<question>_answer` (its text, predictions included), `q_<question>_tries`, `score` (questions right first time), `team_<task>` (shared), `has_<item>` (1 while it is in the student's bag), `twist_<key>`, `away_<character>` (shared), `saved_<rescue>` (shared), `arrival_<scene>` (shared), `c_<experiment>_<control>` (what each control of an instrument was left at), `mission_complete`. `curriculum.evidence` lists, for each objective, its questions (debrief ones marked), predictions and experiments; `curriculum.scored_questions` is there too.

## Stories

When the teacher asks for a story to be told rather than a mission to be played ("Tell the story of a raindrop's trip round the water cycle", "a fable about sharing", "a day in the life of a Roman child"), the generator makes a **story**: an animated picture book that plays by itself, scene after scene, for students to watch and then talk about. The aligner decides which was asked for (`format` in the alignment; `--format=` or the Generate page's **Make** choice decide it instead), and the run keeps it in its options.

A story's blueprint (`StorySchema`, checked by `StoryValidator`) has characters, a narrator's voice, a few props, and scenes, each with the same look as a mission's scene (kind, background, width, far view, foreground, floor, living sky, lights, the air, creatures, and the weather and water below) plus:

- **`cast`**: who is on stage when the scene opens, and where they stand;
- **`script`**: what happens, in order. A beat is `narrate` (a caption, read aloud by the narrator), `say` (a line in a speech bubble, `to` someone, who they turn to face), `walk`, `enter` (from the left or right edge), `leave` (off an edge), `gesture`, `wait`, `sound` (one of [the game's own sounds](/docs/developers/actions/audio/#the-games-own-sounds)), `weather` (the rain starts, a storm blows up, it clears), `pass` (one of the scene's creatures or vehicles goes by now) or `title` (words across the screen). `meanwhile` starts the next beat at once (someone walks across while another speaks).

And an `ending`: a closing line and one to three questions to talk about.

The validator follows each script with who is on the stage, so nobody speaks, walks or gestures before coming on, nobody comes back on after leaving the scene, every character appears somewhere, every scene has something said, and weather and water are only where they make sense. Corrections are made by changes, as for missions.

`StoryPlan::forArt` shows the art director the story as it reads a mission (each character in the first scene they are in, the gestures the script asks for as a cutscene's), so every picture is drawn by the same code, in the same style. **StoryBuilder** (a MissionBuilder, with its helpers) makes every scene a [story scene](/docs/developers/extensions/atmosphere/#story-scenes) and its script one start event that acts it out: characters wait off stage beyond the edge they will come on from, walk on, about and off, turn to whoever they speak to, speak in bubbles; the narrator's captions are read aloud in the narrator's voice (`narrator_voice`, recorded with the characters' lines); the weather changes; a creature or vehicle the script cues (`cue`) passes once when it says. The first scene opens with the title; each ends by jumping to the next; the last ends with "The End", an award, and a box with the questions to talk about and **Watch it again**. Its checklist is one line, "Watch the story to the end" (`mission_complete`). The README lists the scenes and the questions.

## Reading the backgrounds

A painted picture never matches what was asked for to the pixel, so after drawing, each background is surveyed (`SurveyStep`; off with `QBL_GEN_SURVEY=false`, and for placeholder pictures unless `survey` is passed):

- **The floor.** `SceneSurveyor` sends the picture with a labelled grid and asks for the back edge of the floor people could walk on, left to right: along the base of buildings, walls, fences, hedges, cliffs and the water's edge, as far back as anyone could walk on open ground. `FloorTracer` moves each point (and points between, along level stretches) onto the strongest painted line within 3% of the picture's height, 6 pixels in front of it. The walkable area is that edge down to the bottom of the floor; everything placed in the scene (characters, spawn points, things at the back of the floor, the doorway of a top exit) stands in front of it.
- **Things people walk around.** Trees, bushes, lamp posts, fountains, rocks, crates, tables, benches, carts... standing out on the floor (not the buildings, not what hangs on walls, not what lies flat), and railings or fences that run *across* the floor from the front towards the back (diagonal or nearly upright in the picture; one along the back is the floor's edge). The surveyor names each, gives its kind (`SceneSurveyor::KINDS`, now with `railing`), a loose box from its top to its lowest foot, an outline following its legs down, and **how the scene shows through it** (`openings`; if it does not say, `ObjectCutter::openingsFor` reads the name, then the kind):

  openings | for | what inside its outline is cut out with it
  --- | --- | ---
  `none` | a crate, a barrel, a rock, a statue, a pillar, a post | everything: anything the flood hollowed out is filled back. Only a shadow lying beside it on the floor stays behind.
  `under` | a table, a chair, a bench, a cart, a stall, a well with a roof | the thing; a patch it encloses is opened when it is plainly the scenery's colour (the floor between its legs, the wall under its seat), never all of it.
  `lattice` | a railing, a fence, a gate, a free-standing ladder, a bike rack | its bars and posts; every gap that looks like the scenery is opened.
  `leafy` | a tree, a bush, a plant | leaves and trunk; only clear gaps of some size (sky through the canopy) are opened, never the canopy itself.

  `ObjectCutter` floods the scenery in from the edges of a region a little bigger than the box. It stops at outlines: thin dark ink, or a jump in colour (a smaller jump counts in the dark). What the flood cannot reach is the thing. In order:
  - **Sealing.** The outlines are first thickened by a pixel, so a soft or broken outline does not let the flood hollow the thing out. If what is left does not fill the surveyor's outline well enough (across each row, for open things), it tries again with the scenery's own colours as a second wall, then with thicker seals.
  - **Thin parts.** A leg, a baluster or a bare branch that is nothing but ink is joined on.
  - **Scenery lines.** A straight line of the scenery crossing the whole region (a wall's base behind a table, the floor's edge behind a tree) is traced from the region's edge, through the gap where the thing hides it, out the far side, and left in the background. Where such a line crosses a leg, with the thing on both sides of it, the leg goes on across it.
  - **Openings.** Every patch the thing encloses is compared with the scenery's colours just around it and the thing's own colours, and opened or kept by `openings` (above). A darker patch of the floor's own colour, low and flat at its base, is a shadow and stays on the floor, for every kind.
  - **Edges.** Its outermost two pixels each way get the alpha of the blend painted there, from the scenery's colour beside them and the thing's. They also get the thing's own colour, with the scenery taken out. So the cut-out has a clean soft edge over whatever walks behind it and no ring of the old background; everything further in is the background's own pixels.

  A box that stops short (the thing runs out of the region) makes the region grow on that side and the cut run again. When no try fills the surveyor's outline (a dark console on a dark floor, nothing painted between them), everything inside that outline comes too, but for the openings found (`how`: `outline+drawn` or `colour+drawn`): a sliver of floor with it is less wrong than part of it missing. The cut-out is an even number of pixels wide, placed exactly over itself as an item (`scale` undoing the scene's depth there, so it is drawn at 1:1). Its sort line is found as the editor's **✨ Find it from the picture** finds it (`SortLine`, a port of `findSortLine`). A thing on a narrow base under something wider (a tree) gets a level line along its base, and a thing open beneath or between (`under`, `lattice`) gets a line joined from foot to foot (`SortLine::bridged`), not one that rides up the underside of its top over the gaps. Its base is a blocked patch; a railing running across the floor is blocked all along its base. The survey's note lists each thing's openings, how it was cut and how much of it is see-through.
- **What was painted in.** Doors, gates, caves, portals, ladders and stairs up are now painted into the background where they go, and so are things on walls that stay there (a map, a gauge, a fireplace) and lights hung on walls; only holes, hatches and stairs down are still drawn apart, and a wall thing that is collected (it must be able to disappear). The surveyor finds each, and its hotspot (invisible but for its name and question mark) goes over it; a wall light's glow goes on the brightest part of where it was found.

## Little life from the asset library

A scene's `ambient` creatures become the game's [critters](/docs/developers/extensions/atmosphere/#critters): birds that land (`count` for a flock), a squirrel that `climbs` one of the scene's cut-out trees, a rabbit that `hides` between its cut-out bushes, walkers, fliers, swimmers and vehicles. Shy ones scatter when someone comes near (`shy: false` for a tame hen). The designer picks a ready-made animal or vehicle from the asset library (`ambient.library`: `LibraryCritters::CATALOGUE`, the QuestLab Assets) when one fits, and its sheet and animations are used as they are, scaled to its size; only what the library lacks is drawn. `QBL_GEN_LIBRARY_CRITTERS=false` draws everything; the library is `QBL_LIBRARY_DIR`, else the checkout's `scenarios/assets/library`.

## Cutscenes, shots and panels

Stories and missions can be told like a comic or a film (`Design/Cutscenes`):

- **`shot`**: `wide` (the whole place) or `close` (a close-up: the background painted as if the camera were near, everyone drawn twice as big).
- **`continues`**: the next panel of an earlier scene, the same place from the same view a moment later, everyone where they were. With no `changes` the earlier picture is used again (nothing drawn); with `changes` ("the raven now perches on the bookshelf") it is redrawn as an edit of the earlier picture, sent as the first reference; a change of shot draws a close-up of the place from it.
- **`show`** beat: an insert, a picture full-screen for a moment ([a story panel](/docs/developers/actions/image-overlay/#story-panels)) with narration over it, a slow camera move and a `memory` or `dream` tone. Drawn; at most six per story or mission.
- **`camera`** beat: `push_in` on someone, `pull_back` to the stage, `pan` to a spot.
- **A mission's `cutscenes`** (at most two): `when` is `intro` (the first arrival, before the briefing; the validator keeps it under 45 seconds, so students play within a couple of minutes) or an interaction (right after a student first completes it). Each has one to four panels, set `at` one of the mission's places or continuing the panel before; each panel is a story scene of its own (`cut_<key>_<n>`), the last one bringing the student back (beside the interaction, spawn `after-<key>`). Once per student (`seen_cut_<key>`).

The 20-second rule: an insert stays at most 20 seconds whatever is asked; a close-up's script must fit in about 20 seconds (the validator asks for the rest to continue in another panel); and while any story scene plays, `StoryStaging` lets the camera drift slightly whenever nothing has moved for about 14 seconds. The designer is given storytelling devices to choose from: establish then go close, the reveal, before and after, the insert, flashback and imagination, dramatic irony, rising tension, cross-cutting, the cliffhanger.

## Weather, water and vehicles

Missions and stories alike can use them, where the place has them:

- **`weather`** on an outdoor or up-high scene: `rain`, `snow`, `storm`, `blizzard` or `fog`, `light`, `steady` or `heavy`, drawn by the game with its sound ([Weather](/docs/developers/extensions/atmosphere/#weather)). The background is painted in the weather's light (wet ground and puddles, snow lying), never with the rain itself.
- **`water`** in an outdoor or underground scene: a `river` or `stream` across the `front` or the `back` of the floor (which way it `flows`), a `pond` or `lake` in its `middle`, a `waterfall` at the back, the `sea` along the back. The background is asked to paint the water's surface as a flat chroma colour between its banks; it is cut out (`AssetDirector::waterHole` finds where), and the game's [flowing water](/docs/developers/extensions/atmosphere/#flowing-water) is drawn behind it there. If no hole is found after the last attempt, the water is drawn as glints over the picture instead. Nobody walks in it: the floor stops at a river along the front or starts after one along the back, and a pond is blocked off. The validator keeps water clear of passages and of exits it would cut off.
- **`drives_by`** for an ambient thing: a car, a cart, a bus, a tractor driving across the back of the floor (up to 900 cm long), outdoors. Any ambient thing can have a `sound` it makes as it passes (`car`, `hooves`, `tweet`...).

## Curriculum standards

Missions are only aligned to standards in the `curriculum_standards` table: the codes the model may choose from are an enum built from that table, so a code is never invented. Import standards first:

```sh
php artisan qbl:standards                                   # what is imported
php artisan qbl:standards file standards.json --framework=MY-DISTRICT-SCI
php artisan qbl:standards learning-commons <framework uuid> --framework=CA-MATH --grade=5
php artisan qbl:standards csp <standard set id> --framework=NGSS
```

- **A JSON file** needs no account: `[{"code": "...", "description": "...", "grades": ["05"], "subject": "Science"}]`.
- **Learning Commons** (`LEARNING_COMMONS_API_KEY`, from platform.learningcommons.org) has every US state's standards and CCSS, with CASE identifiers.
- **Common Standards Project** (`CSP_API_KEY`) serves standard sets by id.

Importing again updates rows in place. Without any standards for the grade and framework, a mission is still made, with learning objectives but no alignment, and the command says so.

## Keeping every picture in one style

This is the part that takes the most care. Five things work together:

1. **A style bible.** `resources/art-styles/<style>/style.php` says how pictures look: medium, outlines, shading, shapes, proportions, lighting, a named palette, what each kind of picture shows and how it is framed, and what to avoid. `ArtStyle` writes **every** prompt from it, in the same words and the same order, with only the subject changing.
2. **Anchors.** A few approved pictures (an environment, a character, a set of props, a portrait) in `anchors/` go with every request as references, and every result is **judged against them** by a vision model acting as art director (`StyleJudge`): line, shading, colour, shapes, rendering, subject and composition, each out of 10. A picture under the bar (7 by default, and no criterion under 5) is drawn again with the judge's corrections added. After the last attempt the best one is kept and flagged in the README and the command's output.
3. **Design before motion.** Each character is drawn once as a character sheet. Every animation strip is drawn *from* that sheet (it is the first reference), judged against it, and colour-matched to it, and their portrait is cut from it, so a character looks the same in every frame and in the story box. The mission's first background goes with every later background, and its first character with every later character.
4. **One finish.** Cut-outs are drawn on a flat chroma colour (`#FF00FF`) and keyed out (or drawn transparent, where the provider can), trimmed, and scaled to fixed sizes (characters on 240 × 320 frames, items to their real size, passages to fixed heights such as a door at 360 pixels, portraits 320 square on the same backdrop, backgrounds 2250 × 1200). Every picture is pulled part of the way towards the palette (`post.palette_pull`), keeping its shading.
5. **A trained model, later.** Once 15-30 approved pictures exist (the anchors and the best of the generated ones), train a style model on them in Scenario and use `--images=scenario`: the most consistent option of all.

### The styles

- **`qbl-adventure`** (the default): hand-painted cartoon art in the spirit of classic 1990s point-and-click adventure games: vibrant, intricate, whimsical, with lots of open floor. Written from a prompt that gave good results in Recraft.
- **`qbl-cartoon`**: a flatter, cleaner vector cartoon with cel shading.

A new style is a new folder with a `style.php`; copy one of these and change the words.

### Setting up the anchors

Do this once, before generating missions with a real image service (the command refuses otherwise, unless `--without-anchors`):

```sh
php artisan qbl:art-style qbl-adventure --bootstrap                 # 3 candidates each for environment, character, props
php artisan qbl:art-style qbl-adventure --approve=environment:2 --approve=character:1 --approve=props:3
php artisan qbl:art-style qbl-adventure --bootstrap --role=portrait # drawn from the approved character
php artisan qbl:art-style qbl-adventure --approve=portrait:1
```

The candidates are in the work folder under `anchors/`. **Commit the approved anchors** with the style: they define it. In Docker, approving writes into the container; copy them out with `docker compose cp app:/app/resources/art-styles ./apps/api/resources/` and commit them.

Changing the bible or the anchors changes every picture made after it, so bump the style's `version` when you do; every picture records the version it was made with.

The platform's avatars (in `scenarios/assets/platform/`) and the demo's art were made before this style existed and do not match it yet.

### Animations from AutoSprite

[AutoSprite](https://www.autosprite.io) is the primary source of the characters' animations whenever `AUTOSPRITE_API_KEY` is set (a paid plan with API access). Image models draw a good character once and a poor walk cycle, because every frame is a new drawing; AutoSprite animates one picture of the character instead, so every frame is the same character. The character sheet itself is still drawn by the image service and judged against the style anchors, so the cast stays in the house style. Then, for each character (`Art/Providers/AutoSpriteAnimator.php`):

1. The accepted sheet is uploaded as an AutoSprite character (`POST /characters`, free).
2. **One request asks for every animation** (`POST /characters/{id}/spritesheets`): `idle`, `walk`, `wave`, `cheer` (AutoSprite's `victory`), `point`, `think` (a custom animation), `talk`, `jump`, `dance`, `crouch`. Ten is the most one request may hold. The mission's own (walk and the gestures it plays) always come first, so a trimmed `QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_ANIMATIONS` still has them; the rest are there for teachers to use when they edit the mission.
3. The jobs are polled (about 10 seconds, then every 5), and each finished sheet and its atlas are downloaded and cut into frames.

**That request is never made again for the character**: not when an animation fails, not when a job is slow, not on `--resume`, and not by the HTTP client's retries (it is sent without retries, as a retry after a timeout could start, and pay for, a second generation). It is written down in `assets/autosprite-<key>.json` *before* it is sent. If the answer is lost, the character's jobs are looked up rather than asked for again. Everything after the request is a read, which costs nothing. The only way to ask again is to draw the character again (`--redo=draw`, which starts a fresh `assets/`).

What comes back is made into an ordinary QBL sheet, one animation per row, in 256 × 320 cells (16 frames make a 4096 px row, the most phones load). Frames are placed by AutoSprite's pivot (where the feet stand), all at one scale, so a jump stays in the air and raised arms stay raised. The body is as tall as a drawn character's unless the highest jump or widest reach would leave the cell; then the whole character is drawn smaller and `body_height` on the asset tells `MissionBuilder::characterScale` how tall it came out, so the game still shows them at their real size. Idle and walk loop; the rest play once (`talk` and `dance` are made to loop seamlessly, for the editor).

When something is missing: a walk that failed is made from the standing picture and flagged, a missing idle is the standing picture, and a missing gesture is left to the built-in move of that name. Jobs not finished within `QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_TIMEOUT` (900 s) are named in the manifest; resuming the run collects them by polling. If AutoSprite refuses the request outright (no credits, no plan: nothing is spent), the strips are drawn as below, or with `QBL_GEN_ANIMATION_FALLBACK=puppet` nothing more is asked of anyone. With `--offline`, only what was fetched before is used. The upload, the request and its answer, each failed job and each sheet are notes in the run's trace (`calls/NNNN-note-autosprite-*.json`).

Setting | Default | What it does
------- | ------- | ------------
`AUTOSPRITE_API_KEY` | | Turns AutoSprite on. Keys start with `vspk_`.
`QBL_GEN_ANIMATION_DRIVER` | `autosprite` | `draw` to draw strips even with a key.
`QBL_GEN_ANIMATION_FALLBACK` | `draw` | When refused: `draw` the strips, or `puppet`.
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_ANIMATIONS` | all ten | Comma-separated list to ask for.
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_TIER` | `turbo` | `turbo` (5 credits, 2 s), `pro` (15, 4 s), `max` (35), `ultra` (10 a second).
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_FRAMES` | 16 | Frames per animation, 2-16.
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_FRAME_SIZE` | 256 | AutoSprite's frame size, 32-512.
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_REMOVE_BG` | `default` | Or `ultra`, AutoSprite's AI background removal.
`QBL_GEN_AUTOSPRITE_TIMEOUT` | 900 | Seconds to wait for a character's animations.

Not yet run against the live API: the request follows AutoSprite's published reference (`/docs/api-spritesheets`, `/docs/api-jobs`).

### Sprite sheets (drawn strips)

When AutoSprite is not set up (or refuses):

Each character gets `walk` (6 frames), with their standing picture as `idle` (or a drawn 4-frame idle, with `QuestLab_GEN_IDLE_FRAMES=true`), plus the gestures the designer chose that the mission plays (`wave` for a character students talk to, `cheer` for the guide). **Every character walks, stands and has a portrait**, whatever happens: a walk that cannot be drawn in six frames is tried in four, and failing that it is made from the standing picture (it bobs, leans and swings its legs). The portrait is the head and shoulders cut from the character sheet (or drawn, with `QuestLab_GEN_PORTRAITS=draw`; one that cannot be drawn is cut from the sheet and flagged in the README). Each animation is drawn as one strip, from the character sheet. The strip is cut out and its figures found by connected regions (pieces such as a separately drawn hand are joined to their figure); a strip with the wrong number of figures is drawn again. Each frame is colour-matched to the sheet, scaled so the character keeps one size across animations, centred by its centre of mass and stood on a common baseline, then laid out one animation per row as an ordinary QuestLab sheet with `sheet` and `animations` (see [Animations](/docs/developers/extensions/animations/)). A gesture that cannot be drawn well is left out, and the built-in move of that name is used instead.

## One scene for a scenario

The editor's **✨ Generate a scene** (Scenes page and scene map, `apps/editor/src/views/sceneGenerate.ts`) makes ONE scene for the scenario being edited rather than a mission. It is a run like any other (same queue, credits, progress chip, trace and audit log) whose options say `kind: "scene"`, and it is made by the `scene` method (`Methods/SceneMethod`):

Step | Class | What it does
---- | ---- | ----
design | `SceneDesignStep` → `Design/SceneDesigner` | One language-model call plans the scene, told the request, the scenario's title and its other scenes: the background; `standing` (up to 5 things out on the floor to walk around, each with `x`, `depth`, `label`, `look`); `details` (up to 6 things painted in to click); the narrator's `texts` (1-4 passages) and `repeat` (`last` or `cycle`) on details and on standing things worth a look; with life on, `lights` (painted in: `where` wall, back or floor), `sky`, `atmosphere`, `water`, `weather`; with critters on, `ambient`. `SceneDesigner::tidy()` clamps everything, drops what the options turn off, and puts it in the shape of a one-scene mission blueprint, keyed by a shortname no scene of the scenario has.
draw | `SceneDrawStep` | `DrawStep`, as for a mission, with the scenario's start scene (or its first with a picture) passed to the painter as how the game looks (`AssetDirector::lookLike`). The background prompt asks for the standing things with open floor all around each, and the details and lights painted where they go.
survey | `SurveyStep` | As for a mission: the floor traced, the standing things cut out with their sort lines, and the details, lights and described standing things found (`standing:<i>` beside `detail:<i>` and `light:<i>`). Always on for real pictures.
clear | `ClearStep` → `Art/BackgroundCleaner` | The cut-out things taken out of the background itself, so nothing is painted under them (below). The scene's picture becomes `assets/bg-<scene>-cleared.png`; everything the survey measured (floor, sort lines, blocked bases) was measured before, from the picture as painted.
build | `SceneBuildStep` → `Build/SceneBuilder` | The rows of one scene (below). Warnings for anything planned that the picture did not show.
merge | `SceneMergeStep` | `ScenarioStore::mergeRows`: added after the scenario's last scene, its pictures copied into the scenario's media, every id afresh. The run's `scenario_id` is the scenario and its blueprint's `scene_id` the new scene.

What `SceneBuilder` (a `MissionBuilder`, reusing its floor, survey, light, critter, water and sky helpers) makes:

- The scene: the background layer, with a living sky and drifting clouds behind it when it has one; its `sky`, `atmosphere` and `weather` in params.
- `walkable_areas`: the traced floor (`FloorTracer::polygon`), edge to edge unless the plan narrowed it. `blocked_areas`: the base of each cut-out, a pond, and the base of a planned standing thing that could not be cut out. `scale_areas`: one, from the back of the floor as traced (or the ground line) to the bottom, at the sizes the picture was painted for (`Sizes::DEPTH_BACK` at the ground line, carried on behind it). One `default` spawn point in the middle of the floor.
- Each cut-out: an item placed exactly over itself (`placement: object`, `sort_line`, `scale` undoing the depth there, `params.cut_from: background`), named as planned where one matches (by the found box's overlap, else by where across).
- The background, cleared (`BackgroundCleaner`): the holes are each cut-out's own solid pixels, grown by 3 pixels (`GROW`) to take its soft edge and the JPEG ringing beside it. A cut-out's openings (the floor between a bench's legs) are not holes: they are the background's, and stay as painted. Only those pixels change: every other pixel is copied as it was, and the picture is saved as a PNG so not even a JPEG's re-compression touches it. The holes are grouped into 16:9 windows the height of the picture (things side by side share one; a thing wider than a window gets its own). For each window the picture source (`AssetDirector::retouch`, priced into the run's spend like any picture, hints `kind: retouch`) is shown that part of the picture (see-through sky or water in the style's flat colour) and a white-on-black mask of the holes, and asked for the same picture without the things. Its answer is scaled to the window, then checked against a 14-pixel ring of picture around the holes: it is used only if the ring matches (mean difference at most 22 per channel, and at most 12% of the ring off by more than 60 once the overall colour shift is taken away) and the holes changed (at least 10 per channel on average). The holes take its pixels less that colour shift, so they meet the picture without a seam, and its flat colour becomes see-through again. An answer that fails, or a request that fails, is not used: the holes are filled from their edges inwards with the colours around them (fine for flat fills, not for detail behind a big thing), with a warning on the run. `survey/<scene>-cleared.json` records how each window was done and what it was made from, so a resumed run reuses it while the cut-outs are the same. The method decides (`GenerationMethod::clearing()`): `scene` paints (fills, with placeholder pictures); missions and stories keep the things painted in. Option `clear`: `paint`, `fill` or `off`.
- Things to click: a click hotspot over where each was found (else about where it was asked for, with a warning), its label as a hint text (`show_near`), and `narration` actions. With several passages, a player variable `<scene>_look_<n>` counts the clicks: each passage has the condition `= i` (the last `>= n-1` with `last`), then `+ 1` while below `n-1` (`last`), or `+ 1` and `set 0` when `>= n` (`cycle`). Action conditions are read as each action is reached, so the count moves on after the passage is said. A variable the scenario already has is not added again.
- `lights` (on the brightest part of where each was found), `critters` (a squirrel's spots are the cut-out trees, a rabbit's the bushes and rocks; a creature that needs them and has none walks by), `flows` for water.

The API takes `POST editor/generations` with `{kind: "scene", scenario_id, prompt, kind_of_place?, time?, width?, hotspots?, repeat?, animations?, critters?, grade?, notes?}` (`GenerationApi::sceneOptions`: the scenario must be one the teacher can edit; the kind of place is kept as `place`, since `kind` says what is made). It costs `QBL_CREDITS_PER_SCENE` (default 2) and goes through four stages: designing, drawing, building, importing. Runs say `kind` (`mission` or `scene`) and `scene_id`. When one finishes and the editor has its scenario open, `Store.takeIn()` loads the scenario again and records the difference as one history step, so **Ctrl+Z** removes the scene, its media rows, items and variables (the files stay on disk) and **Ctrl+Y** puts them back.

From the command line: `php artisan qbl:generate "A cobbled yard outside an old water mill" --method=scene --scenario=12` (with `--critters`, `--no-hotspots`, `--no-animations`, `--place=indoor`, `--time=dusk`, `--clear=fill` or `--clear=off`, and the usual `--images=placeholder`, `--resume`, `--redo=`).

## From the editor: the queue, progress and cancelling

The editor's **Generate** page (`apps/editor/src/views/generate.ts`) calls `App\Qbl\Generation\GenerationApi` under `/api/editor/generations`. Starting one checks the plan (`Entitlements`), takes the credits (`Credits`) and dispatches `App\Jobs\RunGeneration` to the queue worker in the app container, which runs the same `ScenarioGenerator` as `qbl:generate`.

Route | What
----- | ----
`GET editor/generations` | my runs, the cost, my plan and credits
`POST editor/generations` | start one (202)
`GET editor/generations/active` | the one being made now, or null (the editor's header chip asks every 4 seconds while there is one)
`GET editor/generations/all` | administrators: everyone's latest 50, with who asked and `usd` spent
`GET editor/generations/{id}` | one run
`POST editor/generations/{id}/cancel` | stop it

**Progress** (`GenerationProgress`) is kept on the run's row, `scenario_generations.progress`: the stage, the stages it will go through, the percent, what it is doing, pictures (or lines) done of the total, and when it and the stage started. Each stage owns a slice of the bar weighted by how long it usually takes (drawing is 26-88%). Drawing is counted in the same pictures as the cost estimate (`AssetDirector::estimate`), reported before each one. The bar never goes back. Between reports the editor creeps it forward within the step it is in, never past the next report.

**Cancelling** sets `scenario_generations.cancelled_at`. A run still queued is cancelled at once; a running one checks at every progress report, throws `GenerationCancelled`, and the job marks it `cancelled`. Either way the credits are given back once. A busy run with no progress for 30 minutes (`GenerationApi::STALE_SECONDS`: its worker went away) is marked failed, with its credits back, so the teacher can start another.

Without Docker, the dev server (`npm run dev:editor`) cannot make missions; with `QuestLab_DEV_GENERATOR=1` it pretends, moving a run through every stage in about 70 seconds and ending with an empty draft (a number of seconds above 1 sets how long).

## Testing

`npm run test:scene-generation` runs a scene's whole run with no network, against a stand-in painter (a mill wall, an oak, a well, a lamp, a poster, in the house style) and a stand-in designer and surveyor: the plan tidied, what the painter and surveyor were asked, the four stages, the floor traced to the mill's base, the oak and the well cut out and placed over themselves with their sort lines and bases blocked, the background with them taken out (painted behind them, every other pixel unchanged), `BackgroundCleaner` on its own (filled from around, see-through sky kept, and painter answers that are shifted, unchanged or fail falling back to the fill), the scale area, the hotspots over what they describe, the narrator stepping through to the last passage and going round, the critters, a failed merge leaving the scenario's own files alone, and the game's validator over the result. `QBL_KEEP=1` keeps its work folder.

`npm run test:scenes` covers the survey's parts on pictures in the house style: the floor traced, the tree cut out, and `ObjectCutter`'s rules on a softened room whose wall's base line runs behind everything. The cases: a crate solid through a plank in the wall's colour and a gap in its outline, its shadow left behind; a table whose ink-only legs the wall's base crosses, cut out whole, with the floor, the wall and its shadow between the legs left behind, even from a box that stops short of its feet, and with a sort line from foot to foot; a railing running into the room with every gap open, posts and rails whole, its line climbing with its base and blocked all along it; a tree with the wall showing through a gap in its canopy. Last, a lamp post cut from one picture and laid over another, to check its edge carries none of the first picture's colours.

`npm run test:generation` runs the whole pipeline with no network, and checks every progress report it makes: a stand-in model answers from `apps/api/tests/fixtures/generation/blueprint.php` (a mission with every feature: a door, a hole, a twist, predictions, a debrief, the bag, cutscenes, a wide scene with a far view, foregrounds...), and the placeholder draws the pictures. With the JavaScript side installed (`npm install`), it then checks the generated scenario with the game's own validator, and plays it **from the briefing to the debrief through the real engine** (`apps/client/test/generated-mission.test.ts`): a student goes through the door, picks up twine, sees the twist, digs the channel, jumps into the hole, finds a hook, puts the two together, fishes a battery out of a crack with them, gives it to the robot (after offering him the wrong thing), makes a prediction, runs the experiment, answers the debrief wrong and then right, and watches the pilot leave.

## Reviewing a generated mission

Before publishing, look at:

- `previews/` in the ZIP or the work folder: each scene as it will look, with its walkable floor (green), hotspots (red) and spawn points (blue). Doors, holes and twists' obstacles are drawn too (an obstacle is only there once its twist has happened).
- The README in the ZIP: its standards and objectives, the puzzles with the bag and their answers, the twists, every question (scored, prediction or debrief), and any picture kept below the style bar.
- The mission itself, with the editor's "Test this scene".

Generated content is a first draft from a language model: check facts, reading level and tone as you would anyone else's work.
